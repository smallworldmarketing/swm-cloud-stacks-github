electrical project planning and permits Surrey BC: Cost and Scope Questions

Use a structured question set to uncover scope assumptions, permit responsibilities, dependencies, and exclusions before comparing electrical proposals.

A price is meaningful only when its scope is visible. This checklist helps a Surrey owner decide whether a proposal is defined well enough to compare, revise, or decline. It does not estimate prices or suggest that one contracting model is universally best. Instead, it exposes the assumptions that commonly change project cost: investigation needs, access, restoration, equipment selection, authority requirements, inspection follow-up, utility involvement, and coordination with other trades. Use the same core questions with every candidate, then preserve their answers in writing. The objective is a traceable boundary between included work, allowances, exclusions, owner duties, and changes that cannot yet be priced responsibly.

Questions that establish the priced boundary

Ask the contractor to restate the requested outcome in plain language. Which rooms, tenant areas, circuits, fixtures, outlets, switches, panels, chargers, controls, or pieces of equipment are included? Is the proposal for investigation, design support, installation, repair, replacement, or a combination? Where an existing condition is unknown, ask whether the proposal includes an allowance, a diagnostic visit, or a later quotation after assessment. A confident fixed description of an unseen problem is not the same as evidence. Request quantities and product responsibilities. Identify who selects and supplies fixtures, controls, fans, heaters, devices, charger equipment, panels, or specialty components. For owner-supplied products, ask who checks suitability before installation and what happens if an item is incomplete, damaged, incompatible, unavailable, or not approved for the intended use. Product suitability and electrical compatibility require professional review; a worksheet cannot make those findings. Clarify physical access. Does the price assume exposed framing, accessible ceilings, empty cabinets, clear electrical rooms, or work during ordinary hours? Who moves furniture, merchandise, ceiling tiles, machinery, or stored goods? Ask whether cutting, trenching, fire stopping, concrete work, drywall repair, painting, landscaping, hazardous-material procedures, or weatherproofing are included. For occupied commercial sites, include escorts, security, noise limits, dust controls, shutdown windows, and business-continuity measures. List related parties and approvals. A renovation can involve an owner, tenant, landlord, strata, designer, general contractor, mechanical contractor, utility, and permitting authority. Ask which communications are included and who supplies drawings, equipment schedules, letters, access approval, or utility account authorization. If the project requires a shutdown, do not assume its scheduling or duration. Ask who develops and approves the plan after site assessment. Finally, request a written exclusions list. Useful proposals distinguish known exclusions from unresolved questions. That distinction lets an owner obtain missing information instead of treating uncertainty as a hidden promise.

Permit, change, and closeout questions that affect the total

Determine the authority before discussing permit administration. The City of Surrey and Technical Safety BC publish separate electrical-permit information, so ask the contractor which authority they believe applies and how that will be confirmed. Then ask whether a permit is expected, who will apply, whose name will appear, what fees are included, who schedules inspections, and how corrections or additional visits are handled. Do not ask a contractor to guarantee approval. Authority decisions can depend on the actual submission and inspected work. Examine the change process. What event produces a change proposal? Must the owner approve it before work continues? Will the document show labour, materials, schedule effects, and tax treatment? How will concealed conditions be photographed and explained? Set a named approver, especially for rental, strata, or commercial property. An electrician should still take any action necessary to leave a discovered hazard in a safe state, but broader added work should have a documented authorization path. Ask how the payment schedule corresponds to observable milestones, supplied equipment, permit activity, inspection, and closeout. Avoid using payment structure as a proxy for contractor competence. Compare it alongside verified credentials, scope clarity, communication, and project controls. Record whether pricing expires, whether substitutions require consent, and how unavailable products affect the agreement. Define completion records before booking. Depending on the job, ask about the final invoice, equipment literature, warranties as actually offered, labelled schedules, permit documents, inspection results, approved changes, commissioning information, and a list of remaining items. Never assume a permit is closed because physical work appears finished. Confirm status with the relevant record or authority. A proposal is ready for comparison when another reader can identify the exact outcome, location, included quantities, material responsibilities, access assumptions, exclusions, permit roles, change mechanism, payment milestones, and closeout package. If two bids cover different boundaries, normalize the scope or request revisions rather than choosing from incomparable totals.

Questions this guide answers

Why can two electrical proposals have very different totals?

They may include different quantities, products, access assumptions, restoration, permit administration, investigation, scheduling, or closeout records. Compare boundaries before totals.

Should a quote guarantee permit approval?

No. Ask who manages the application and inspection process, but leave approval decisions to the applicable authority.
