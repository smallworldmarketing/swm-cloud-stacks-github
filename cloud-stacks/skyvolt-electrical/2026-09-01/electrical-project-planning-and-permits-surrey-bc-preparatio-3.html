electrical project planning and permits Surrey BC: Preparation Checklist 2

Prepare a decision-ready electrical project file with eight practical checks covering scope, access, authority, and records.

Good preparation does not mean investigating electrical equipment yourself. It means assembling safe, relevant information so a contractor and the applicable authority can make their respective decisions efficiently. This eight-part checklist is designed for a Surrey home, commercial premises, renovation, tenant improvement, repair discussion, lighting change, wiring project, panel question, or EV-charging proposal. It deliberately avoids energized testing, panel access, code conclusions, and equipment sizing. Complete only items available through ordinary observation and existing records. If there is heat, smoke, arcing, a burning smell, shock, or water exposure, stop the planning exercise, keep people away, discontinue use only if that can be done safely, and seek appropriate emergency or qualified help.

Checks 1–3: outcome, site identity, and safe observations

1. Write a one-sentence outcome. Describe what should be usable or resolved when the project is complete. Examples include lighting for a defined work area, receptacle locations for an approved layout, electrical support for specified tenant equipment, or a professional assessment of a recurring symptom. Avoid prescribing conductor sizes, breaker ratings, panel capacity, or a repair method. Add a second list separating required outcomes from preferences. This gives a contractor room to assess feasible options without losing the owner's priorities. 2. Confirm the site identity and decision makers. Record the complete project address, property type, unit or suite, owner, tenant, strata or manager contact, and person authorized to approve scope and changes. Note lease, strata, heritage, or building-management conditions already known, but do not guess at permissions. For commercial work, identify operational contacts and any security, loading, roof, electrical-room, or after-hours access procedure. For residential projects, list household access constraints, pets, parking, and quiet periods. 3. Capture observations without touching equipment. Record dates, locations, frequency, affected uses, and what was happening when a symptom appeared. Take photographs only from a safe position of visible fixtures, devices, equipment labels, room layouts, or damage. Never remove a cover, open a panel, reset equipment repeatedly, test a conductor, enter a restricted room, or approach wet or visibly damaged electrical components. A symptom log is context, not a diagnosis. If the condition changes or shows an urgent warning sign, escalate rather than continuing documentation.

Checks 4–6: records, authority, and project interfaces

4. Gather existing records. Look for past permits, inspection documents, as-built drawings, panel schedules, equipment manuals, renovation plans, invoices, landlord standards, strata approvals, and previous deficiency reports. Preserve filenames and dates; do not alter an original to make it fit the new project. Mark uncertain records as unverified. A contractor can then distinguish documentary history from current site conditions. 5. Identify the authority question. The City of Surrey maintains electrical-permit information, while Technical Safety BC publishes installation-permit and homeowner-permit guidance. Ask which authority has jurisdiction over the precise address and work. Record the answer, date, contact channel, and any project identifier. Do not decide that work is permit-exempt because it appears small. If considering a homeowner permit, review official eligibility information before purchasing material or arranging help. Permit eligibility and inspection requirements must come from the authority, not this checklist. 6. Map project interfaces. List planned demolition, cabinetry, mechanical equipment, plumbing, data, fire alarm, security, roofing, exterior work, landscaping, painting, and finish restoration that could intersect the electrical scope. Add vehicle charging equipment, appliances, machinery, signs, heaters, fans, controls, or lighting selected by others. Record manufacturer and model information if already chosen, without assuming compatibility. Assign a coordinator for drawings and revisions. Interface mapping helps reveal questions for qualified parties; it does not authorize the owner to design wiring or establish loads.

Checks 7–8: access plan and comparison package

7. Prepare an access and disruption sheet. Show permitted work hours, key or escort arrangements, parking, loading, elevator bookings, ceiling height, known wall finishes, protected areas, tenant or neighbour notices, and rooms that must remain secure. Ask the contractor to define anticipated interruptions and safe isolation arrangements. The owner should communicate business or household priorities but must not direct service work, bypass protection, or restore power against professional advice. Include a contact tree for site decisions and urgent communications without assuming any provider offers emergency attendance. 8. Build one comparison package. Give prospective providers the same outcome statement, site facts, photographs, document list, interface map, timing constraints, and questions. Request a scope that distinguishes included work, exclusions, options, assumptions, permit and inspection responsibilities, restoration, change approval, and closeout records. Ask for current credential details that can be verified through the proper live source. Do not request invented proof such as review scores or guarantees. Before sending the package, remove unrelated personal information, access codes, financial records, and tenant data. Keep a distribution log and version number so later changes are visible. After site reviews, update the file with written clarifications rather than relying on memory. Preparation is complete when providers can assess the same requested outcome and identify remaining investigations. It is not complete merely because every blank is filled; unresolved technical or authority matters should stay visibly unresolved. That distinction protects the decision process from false certainty.

Questions this guide answers

Should preparation include opening the electrical panel for photographs?

No. Do not open panels or covers, test conductors, or approach damaged equipment. Supply records and photographs obtainable from a safe position.

What makes the project package ready to send?

It should state the outcome, site and access facts, available records, interfaces, constraints, and comparison questions while clearly labelling unresolved technical matters.
