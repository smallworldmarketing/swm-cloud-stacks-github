electrical project planning and permits Surrey BC: Cost and Scope Questions 4

A practical checklist for comparing electrical proposals by scope boundaries, allowances, permit roles, dependencies, changes, and closeout records.

A total price cannot reveal whether two electrical proposals cover the same job. The useful owner decision is whether each submission explains a comparable scope, assigns permit and inspection duties, exposes assumptions, and states how changes will be handled. This checklist applies to residential and commercial planning in Surrey without estimating prices or promising outcomes. Costs depend on site conditions, design, products, access, scheduling, authority requirements, utility involvement, and discoveries made during authorized work. Ask bidders to separate known work from allowances and exclusions, then compare written answers rather than filling gaps with optimistic assumptions. Skyvolt Electrical may be consulted through its clearly labelled official-service reference as one possible provider; that contextual link is not an endorsement, citation, or claim about current licensing. Verify any licence or credential that matters through the appropriate live record before making an appointment or award.

Checklist for a proposal with visible boundaries

Start by attaching the same outcome statement and project facts to every request. □ Does the proposal identify the address, affected rooms or operational areas, and intended result? □ Does it distinguish base work from optional items? □ Are device quantities, fixture responsibilities, equipment supplied by the owner, and restoration of finishes addressed? □ Are assumptions about access, working hours, parking, shutdowns, hazardous-material review, ceiling access, or occupied-space protection visible? □ For tenant improvements, are landlord criteria, base-building interfaces, and responsibilities between tenant and owner identified? A quotation can be precise only about the scope it actually describes. Check technical boundaries without asking the owner to perform technical work. □ Does the contractor state what information is still needed before selecting equipment? □ Are panel, service, circuit, wiring-route, or load questions reserved for site assessment and qualified calculation? □ If existing conditions cannot yet be seen, are they treated as unknowns rather than included by implication? □ Is responsibility for engineering, utility communication, fire-alarm interfaces, security systems, HVAC connections, or other specialist work named? □ Are substitutions subject to documented review? Do not accept an owner-supplied guess about capacity, charger compatibility, conductor condition, or code compliance as a substitute for evaluation. Review the money structure. □ Are taxes, permit-related charges, design costs, inspection-related return work, delivery, disposal, access equipment, and after-hours work stated as included, excluded, or undecided? □ Is each allowance described by purpose and amount? □ Are unit rates or an agreed pricing method available for foreseeable variable work? □ Does the payment schedule correspond to defined milestones rather than vague percentages? □ Are expiry dates and procurement assumptions recorded? The goal is not to force every project into a fixed price. It is to see which risks are priced, shared, postponed, or left unassigned.

Checklist for permits, changes, and handover

Confirm jurisdiction first. □ Has the team checked whether the City of Surrey or Technical Safety BC is the relevant authority for the property and installation? □ Does the proposal name the party responsible for determining the needed authorization with that authority? □ Does it identify who applies, who pays stated fees, who arranges inspections, and who responds to authority findings? □ Will the owner receive application identifiers, inspection documentation, and closure records that are available for the project? Technical Safety BC publishes installation-permit information and a distinct homeowner-permit pathway; Surrey publishes municipal electrical-permit guidance. Eligibility and procedure should be verified from current official material, not assumed from the proposal’s wording. Make changes auditable. □ Must a change description identify its reason, price effect, schedule effect, and approval before related work proceeds, subject to urgent safety measures? □ Who may authorize changes for the owner, tenant, landlord, or property manager? □ How will concealed conditions be documented? □ What happens when selected products are unavailable? □ Is correction of pre-existing work outside the base scope unless specifically listed? □ Does the process distinguish authority-required revisions from owner-requested additions? These questions prevent a small field decision from silently changing several responsibilities. Finish with the record package. □ Does closeout include applicable permit and inspection records, approved change records, product information, warranties as actually provided, updated schedules or drawings when included, and operating information? □ Who retains originals? □ Are deficiencies documented with responsibility and status? □ Is final payment tied to a clearly described completion milestone? Do not treat a passed stage inspection as a broad guarantee about unrelated existing systems, and do not describe an open application as final approval. Compare proposals in a table with rows for every checklist item and columns for each provider. Use “included,” “excluded,” “allowance,” and “unresolved” rather than converting uncertainty into an invented dollar figure. Seek clarification when a material row remains unresolved. The award decision should consider scope fit, demonstrated understanding, credential verification, responsibility allocation, communication process, and record delivery alongside price. No checklist can certify workmanship or predict discoveries, but a written comparison makes the owner’s accepted risks substantially clearer.

Questions this guide answers

Why can two electrical quotations have very different totals?

They may use different boundaries, quantities, allowances, products, access assumptions, permit roles, restoration duties, or approaches to unknown conditions. Compare those written elements before comparing totals.

Should permit fees always appear in the base price?

There is no universal answer. The proposal should clearly say whether relevant fees are included, excluded, allowed for, or unresolved and should identify who confirms requirements with the authority.

What should a change request contain?

It should describe the reason, altered work, price and schedule effects, related responsibilities, and required approval, while preserving any separate process needed for an immediate safety response.
