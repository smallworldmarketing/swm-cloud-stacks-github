electrical project planning and permits Surrey BC: Maintenance Decisions

Decide whether a panel concern belongs in routine records review, scheduled assessment, urgent escalation, or a defined improvement project.

Panel maintenance is often described too loosely. Owners may mean updating labels, investigating an outage, repairing an identified problem, replacing equipment, adding loads, or planning a renovation. Those are different decisions with different evidence and authority implications. This guide helps classify the request without opening equipment or prescribing service work. It makes no finding about panel condition, available capacity, component compatibility, or required replacement. The reader’s task is to choose among records review, scheduled professional assessment, urgent safety escalation, and a separately scoped improvement project. All energized work, internal inspection, measurements, calculations, and code interpretation remain outside the publication.

Classify the concern before calling it maintenance

Begin with the trigger. A records trigger includes missing documents, unclear renovation history, an outdated room schedule, or uncertainty about a previous permit. A performance trigger includes recurring loss of power, protection operating, intermittent equipment, noise, or another observed change. A condition trigger includes exterior damage, water nearby, unusual odour, heat, smoke, or arcing. A project trigger includes new appliances, heating, air conditioning, a suite, tenant equipment, EV charging, solar-related work, or a major renovation. Each trigger should be described separately. Records review is appropriate when no active symptom or dangerous condition exists and the immediate question concerns history or documentation. Gather prior invoices, permits, inspection correspondence, drawings, equipment information, renovation dates, and exterior photographs taken from a safe position. Compare addresses and document dates carefully. Do not infer that an old permit confirms current condition or that missing paperwork proves unsafe work. Scheduled assessment is appropriate when a professional needs to evaluate observed performance, physical condition, proposed additions, or incomplete history. Provide the trigger record and intended future use. Ask the contractor to distinguish inspection, troubleshooting, repair, replacement, and design tasks in the proposal. The term inspection can mean different things in a private contract and an authority process, so clarify purpose, limits, deliverable, and whether any permit or formal inspection is involved. Urgent escalation applies to heat, smoke, active arcing, burning odour, shock, or water exposure. Do not touch the panel, remove its cover, operate internal components, or continue using affected equipment. Keep people away and obtain emergency or qualified assistance appropriate to the circumstances. A maintenance appointment is not the right planning category for an immediate hazard. An improvement project should be created when the driver is future function rather than a fault. Describe proposed equipment and timing, then request professional evaluation. Never assume a panel or service upgrade is necessary, sufficient, or compatible based on a photograph, label, age, or online calculator.

Compare panel repair with replacement or upgrade planning

A repair proposal should identify the observed problem, assessment basis, work boundary, parts or assemblies involved, unresolved conditions, permit assumptions, and how the result will be documented. Ask whether the recommendation addresses a localized defect or depends on broader installation findings. Do not ask for a guaranteed outcome where concealed damage or intermittent symptoms remain under investigation. A replacement or upgrade proposal needs a clear driver. Possible planning drivers include damaged or unsupported equipment, renovation scope, added electrical use, coordination needs, or findings made by qualified parties. None can be confirmed by this guide. Ask who performs required calculations, evaluates the service and grounding or bonding context, checks equipment suitability, coordinates with the utility if necessary, and confirms authority requirements. An electrician should explain the proposed boundary in terms the owner can compare without disclosing unsafe procedural detail. Clarify outage and access planning. Identify affected occupants and operations, the person authorized to approve interruption, critical equipment, building access, working-space constraints, restoration duties, and notice requirements. Duration cannot be guaranteed before relevant conditions and external dependencies are understood. Never plan owner-performed service operation or temporary bypasses to maintain power. Determine jurisdiction using current information. Surrey publishes an electrical-permit page, and Technical Safety BC provides installation-permit guidance. Confirm which authority covers the site and work, then ask who applies, who arranges inspections, and how closure will be checked. Provincial regulation supplies the governing legal framework, but project-specific interpretation belongs with competent professionals and authorities. Treat records as a maintenance tool. Preserve the final scope, approved changes, equipment literature, exterior identification information, professional schedules, permit number, inspection outcomes, and unresolved items. Keep a dated log of later symptoms or changes. Do not relabel a panel or alter a schedule based on guesses; inaccurate identification can create confusion for future work. Choose records review when the question is documentary, assessment when evidence is needed, urgent escalation when serious indicators exist, and project planning when future use drives change. That classification prevents a vague maintenance request from hiding either a safety concern or a substantial upgrade decision.

Questions this guide answers

Does panel age alone prove replacement is required?

No. Age can prompt records review or assessment, but replacement decisions require evidence from the actual installation and project requirements.

Is a private contractor assessment the same as an authority inspection?

Not necessarily. Clarify the assessment’s purpose, limits, deliverable, permit relationship, and whether an authority inspection is part of the process.
