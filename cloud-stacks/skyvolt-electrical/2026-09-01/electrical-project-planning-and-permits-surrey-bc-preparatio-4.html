electrical project planning and permits Surrey BC: Preparation Checklist 3

A pre-visit checklist for safely collecting project records, access details, fixture schedules, operating constraints, approvals, and closeout expectations.

Good preparation makes a site conversation more productive without asking an owner to investigate electrical equipment. The goal is to assemble facts already available, document the desired result, and make safe access and decision authority clear. Do not remove panel covers, touch exposed components, test conductors, defeat protection, or continue using equipment associated with heat, smoke, arcing, burning odour, shock, or water exposure. Those conditions belong on an immediate safe-escalation path. For routine project planning, use this checklist before a contractor visit or proposal request, then mark unknown items honestly rather than guessing.

Property, scope, and access checklist

□ Write a one-sentence objective for each area. Examples include revising lighting for a new workspace, relocating outlets after a verified layout change, scoping an EV charging project, or coordinating electrical work within a tenant improvement. Describe the result rather than selecting conductor, breaker, service, or panel specifications. □ Prepare a marked floor plan, lease plan, or simple sketch. Show rooms, fixture points, switches, receptacles, equipment, parking positions, outdoor areas, and preferred control locations. Label dimensions as approximate unless measured reliably. Include photographs taken from normal occupied areas only; do not enter restricted spaces or expose electrical parts. □ Create a fixture and equipment schedule. Record manufacturer and model information already visible on packaging, manuals, or ordinary nameplates that can be read safely. Note who purchases each item, expected delivery, mounting location, colour or finish, controls, and any return deadline. Product selection does not confirm electrical compatibility. □ Gather existing records. Look for prior permit documents, inspection correspondence, renovation drawings, panel schedules, equipment manuals, strata rules, landlord approvals, utility correspondence, and invoices. Preserve original dates and identify anything believed incomplete. Do not represent an old record as proof of current condition. □ List property constraints. Include occupied suites, business hours, customer routes, mobility requirements, pets, alarms, locked rooms, ceiling type, heritage or finish concerns, exterior gates, loading rules, parking control, elevator bookings, and noise limitations. Identify the person who can authorize access. □ Note other project participants. List the general contractor, designer, property manager, strata contact, landlord, equipment vendor, mechanical contractor, security or alarm provider, drywall contractor, painter, and utility contact when relevant. Record who coordinates each interface instead of assuming the electrician will manage every dependency.

Authority, visit, and closeout checklist

□ Confirm the address and jurisdiction. Ask whether the City of Surrey or Technical Safety BC is the applicable electrical authority for the specific work. Use current official guidance as a reference, but leave the actual permit determination to the authority and responsible qualified party. □ Ask who will hold and administer any required permit. Record who submits the application, arranges inspections, receives notices, manages corrections, and provides final documentation. If a homeowner-permit route is proposed, check the current eligibility criteria directly. Ownership alone should not be treated as authorization. □ Set a safe site-visit plan. Ensure ordinary access paths are clear, keys and escorts are arranged, parking instructions are supplied, and relevant decision-makers can attend or respond. Do not clear storage where doing so would bring anyone near exposed conductors or visibly damaged equipment. Tell the contractor beforehand about known water entry, unusual odours, past overheating, shocks, or other safety concerns so they can direct the next safe action. □ Prepare operational questions. For a residence, note essential occupancy needs, remote work, medical considerations that the household chooses to disclose, heating, refrigeration, and access limitations. For a business, identify critical systems, permitted shutdown windows, tenant notices, production schedules, network requirements, and after-hours rules. These facts help a contractor plan; they do not establish outage duration. □ Decide how proposals will be compared. Request the same scope structure from each provider: inclusions, exclusions, allowances, assumptions, permit responsibility, restoration, coordination, change process, schedule conditions, and completion records. Avoid asking for a single unexplained total. □ Define closeout expectations. Depending on scope, request permit and inspection records, approved changes, final equipment information, manuals, applicable commissioning records, updated circuit information included in the contract, and photographs of concealed work. Specify file format and recipient. □ Verify current credentials in the appropriate live record. Record the legal contracting identity and relevant licence information provided, then check it rather than relying on advertising or an undated copy. The checklist is complete when a contractor can understand the desired outcome, visit safely, see material constraints, identify the authority question, and state what remains to be investigated. Unknown technical conditions are expected. Keeping them visible is more useful than filling gaps with assumptions.

Questions this guide answers

Should I open a panel to photograph it before a site visit?

No. Never remove a cover or approach exposed electrical parts. Provide records and ordinary-area photographs, and leave equipment inspection to qualified personnel.

What records are useful before requesting an electrical proposal?

Prior permits, inspection correspondence, drawings, schedules, manuals, approvals, utility correspondence, and dated invoices can help when clearly identified as historical.

Should unknown technical details be guessed on the checklist?

No. Mark them unknown so the contractor can identify what assessment is required.
