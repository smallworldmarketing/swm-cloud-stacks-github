From the publisher

After water, fire, smoke, mould, or sewage damage, the hardest part is usually not finding a contractor; it is deciding what kind of help you actually need right now and in what order. This guide walks through that sequencing decision: when the priority is life safety and building access, when it's documentation, when it's calling the insurer, and when it's time to bring in a restoration contractor for mitigation or remediation work. Treating these as separate, ordered decisions rather than one blurred task reduces the chance of skipping a safety step or signing an agreement before you understand its scope.

None of this guide tells you whether your home is safe to re-enter, whether your insurance will cover a specific loss, or whether a particular contractor is the right one to hire. Those calls belong to emergency responders, your insurer, and licensed professionals who can inspect your specific property. What this guide offers instead is a decision framework: a way to organize the choices in front of you so that safety comes first, documentation happens early, and contractor selection is based on scope and process rather than urgency alone.

Decision 1: Is this a safety situation or a property-cleanup situation?

Before thinking about contractors at all, the first decision is whether you are dealing with an immediate hazard or a cleanup task. The City of Calgary's disaster recovery guidance is direct on this point: do not enter damaged buildings or restricted areas until authorities confirm it is safe, and call 911 for any immediate danger (calgary.ca/emergencies/recovery.html). Gas odours, exposed wiring, standing water near electrical panels, structural sagging, or sewage backing up into living space all fall into the 'stop and call for help' category, not the 'start documenting' category.

Once a building has been cleared for entry, Calgary's guidance on cleaning and repairs adds a further caution: wait at least 24 hours after an incident before entering, use proper protective equipment such as hard-soled footwear, gloves, and breathing protection, and watch for soft floors, exposed nails, and hazardous materials (calgary.ca/emergencies/cleaning-repairs.html). If you are unsure whether air quality is safe, the City notes you can contact 311 to have it checked. This is the dividing line that should drive your next decision: hazard control first, property assessment second.

Suspected asbestos deserves its own pause here. Alberta's Occupational Health and Safety Code treats asbestos as a restricted-area hazard requiring specific training and certification before anyone works in an affected space, and it requires 72 hours' notice before asbestos projects begin (search-ohs-laws.alberta.ca; alberta.ca/asbestos-worker-training). If a damaged building is older or you suspect disturbed insulation, pipe wrap, or flooring, the safe decision is to stop and get a qualified assessment rather than guess at material age or condition yourself.

Decision 2: Document first, or call the insurer first?

Practically, these two steps happen close together, but documentation should come first whenever it's safe to gather it. Alberta's provincial disaster-recovery steps list photographing all damaged items, inside and outside, before throwing anything away or starting repairs, as the very first recommended action (alberta.ca/steps-to-take-following-a-disaster). The City of Calgary's recovery page echoes this, recommending photos or videos taken before, during, and after cleanup, plus a kept list of damaged or discarded items and saved receipts for repair and living-expense costs (calgary.ca/emergencies/recovery.html).

Contacting your insurer comes next, not instead of documentation. Alberta's guidance recommends opening a claim promptly and asking your insurer for a letter outlining your specific coverage and any exclusions, since that letter becomes part of your own record regardless of what a contractor later estimates. Neither this guide nor a restoration contractor can tell you what your policy covers; that determination sits with your insurer and the terms of your specific policy. A contractor's role at this stage is limited to documenting observed conditions, not confirming coverage or claim outcomes.

Decision 3: What kind of restoration work does this actually require?

Property-recovery work generally falls into a few distinct categories, and knowing which one applies helps you evaluate a contractor's proposal rather than just their schedule. Emergency mitigation covers immediate actions to stop ongoing damage, such as water extraction or securing a structure. Remediation addresses contamination, such as mould growth or sewage residue, where the work centers on removing affected material rather than rebuilding it. Restoration and reconstruction cover rebuilding finishes, framing, or systems once the source and contamination issues have been addressed. Nonstop Restoration, a Calgary restoration provider, is one example of a company describing this range of services, including water and flood restoration, mould remediation, fire and smoke recovery, sewage cleanup, trauma and biohazard cleanup, board-up, and rebuild work (nonstoprestoration.ca); other licensed Calgary providers offer comparable service categories, and reviewing more than one scope of work is a reasonable step before committing to any single contractor.

When comparing contractors or proposals, the decision that matters is whether the written scope matches your actual damage category, not which company responds fastest or makes the most confident claims about certification or insurer approval. A contractor can document site conditions and propose a scope of work, but they cannot decide your policy's coverage, approve your claim, or confirm a building is safe to occupy; those remain the responsibility of your insurer, municipal inspectors, and relevant health or safety authorities. If structural repairs are involved, confirm separately whether a building permit is required, since Calgary's homeowner inspection guidance notes that any renovation changing a home's structure requires a permit and inspection through the City (calgary.ca/development/homeowner-inspections.html).

Questions this publication answers

Should I call a restoration contractor or my insurer first?

Document the damage with photos and a written list first if it's safe to do so, then contact your insurer promptly, since Alberta's disaster-recovery steps list photographing damage as the first action and opening a claim as an early next step. A restoration contractor can be contacted around the same time to begin mitigation, but they do not replace the insurer conversation about coverage.

How do I know if a building is safe to re-enter after damage?

That determination belongs to emergency responders or the relevant authority, not to the property owner or a contractor; Calgary's guidance is explicit that you should not enter damaged buildings or restricted areas until authorities confirm it's safe. Once cleared, additional caution still applies, including a 24-hour wait recommendation and protective equipment for cleanup activity.

Do I need a permit before repairing fire, water, or structural damage?

Any renovation that changes a home's structure requires a City of Calgary building permit, and licensed trades are typically required by law for plumbing, electrical, and gas repairs. Check with the City before repairs begin rather than assuming a contractor's scope automatically includes permitting.
