Three Calgary Loss Scenarios: Scope Questions Compared

Compare how cost and scope questions differ across a basement flood, a kitchen fire, and a sewage backup in Calgary before signing any work order.

A property owner reading one restoration estimate often assumes the questions they should ask are the same no matter what caused the damage. They are not. A basement flood, a kitchen fire, and a sewage backup each raise different scope questions, different exclusions, and different documentation needs, and treating them identically can leave gaps in a claim file or an authorization form.

This article walks through three realistic Calgary scenarios side by side so a reader can see which scope and cost questions apply to their own situation. It does not estimate prices, promise outcomes, or tell anyone which contractor to choose. It is a comparison of what to ask and document, built around the kind of decisions a homeowner, landlord, or property manager actually faces before authorizing work.

None of these scenarios replace guidance from the City of Calgary, Alberta Health Services, or an insurer. They are a structured way to think through scope before money changes hands.

Scenario one: a basement flood from a burst pipe

A burst pipe flood typically raises questions about materials and drying, not just cleanup. A reader comparing estimates for this scenario should ask what materials are being removed versus dried in place, since drywall, insulation, and some flooring may need replacement while framing can sometimes be dried and monitored instead. Ask for the specific rooms and square footage included in the scope, because a quote that lists 'basement water mitigation' without measurements is hard to compare against a second quote.

Cost and scope questions for this scenario should also cover equipment duration. A contractor may propose a number of days for air movers and dehumidifiers, but the actual drying time depends on moisture readings, not a fixed schedule. Alberta's guidance on post-disaster recovery recommends documenting damage with photos before cleanup begins and keeping receipts for all clean-up and repair costs, which matters regardless of which contractor is chosen (alberta.ca, steps to take following a disaster).

A reader should also confirm whether the scope includes contents handling separately from structural drying, since movers, cleaners, and structural technicians are sometimes billed as distinct line items. If a quote bundles everything together, ask for an itemized breakdown before comparing it to another bid.

Scenario two: a kitchen fire with smoke spread to other rooms

A kitchen fire scenario shifts the scope questions toward surfaces, air, and contents rather than drying equipment. The City of Calgary's guidance on cleaning and repairs after an emergency notes that anything beyond minor smoke damage is often better handled by a professional cleaning service or restoration contractor, and that affected items may need to be kept for insurance purposes even if they look ruined (calgary.ca, cleaning and repairs after an emergency, updated November 4, 2026). A reader comparing quotes should ask which rooms are included in odour and soot treatment, since smoke can travel through HVAC systems well beyond the room where the fire occurred.

Scope comparison here should include whether contents cleaning is quoted per item, per box, or as a flat allowance, because these pricing structures are not directly comparable without a shared inventory list. Ask whether structural odour treatment is included or billed separately from surface cleaning, and whether any demolition of fire-damaged materials requires a building permit before repairs begin, a step the City of Calgary says should be confirmed through 311 before renovation work starts.

A reader should also ask what happens to items that cannot be cleaned, since disposal decisions can affect an insurance claim if they happen before the adjuster has reviewed the loss.

Scenario three: a sewage backup in a rental unit

A sewage backup scenario introduces contamination and access questions that do not apply to the first two scenarios. Because sewage contact involves biological hazards, Alberta's Occupational Health and Safety Code addresses worker exposure to harmful substances and decontamination requirements in restricted-condition work (Part 4, Chemical Hazards, Biological Hazards and Harmful Substances, Occupational Health and Safety Code). A tenant or landlord comparing scope here should ask who is responsible for restricting access to the affected unit while cleanup is underway, and whether the quote distinguishes between affected and unaffected areas.

Cost and scope comparison for a sewage event should separate disposal of contaminated porous materials, such as carpet or baseboards, from cleaning of hard surfaces, since these follow different handling and documentation paths. Ask whether the scope includes a written record of what was removed and why, since this record matters for both the landlord-tenant relationship and any insurance claim. A landlord should also confirm with the property manager or strata, where applicable, who holds responsibility for authorizing work in a shared or multi-unit building.

Across all three scenarios, the comparison that matters most is not the total price but whether each estimate names its materials, areas, and exclusions clearly enough that a second contractor's bid can be measured against it on the same terms.

Questions this guide answers

Are cost and scope questions the same for every type of property damage?

No, the relevant questions change with the type of loss. A water event centers on materials, drying, and equipment duration, a fire centers on surfaces, air, and contents, and a sewage backup centers on contamination and restricted access, so each requires a different comparison checklist.

Should contents cleaning always be quoted separately from structural work?

It is easier to compare estimates when contents handling is itemized separately from structural drying or demolition, since bundled pricing can hide which service is driving the total cost. Ask for a breakdown if a quote does not already separate these categories.

Who decides whether a sewage-affected area needs restricted access?

Access decisions in a contaminated area should follow guidance from workplace safety rules and the responsible party for the building, such as a landlord, property manager, or strata council, rather than being left to informal judgment. Alberta's Occupational Health and Safety Code addresses exposure and decontamination expectations relevant to this kind of work.
