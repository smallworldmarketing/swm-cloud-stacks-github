Calgary property damage recovery planning: Cost and Scope Questions

A practical checklist for testing quantities, allowances, exclusions, responsibilities, and change controls in a Calgary property-recovery estimate.

A useful recovery estimate explains what will be done, where, by whom, and under which assumptions. The price at the bottom cannot answer those questions. Before authorizing work, convert each proposal into a common checklist that exposes differences in quantities, emergency measures, specialist tasks, contents handling, and rebuilding. This process does not establish what an insurer will pay, and the cheapest or most detailed document is not automatically the right choice. Its purpose is to reveal uncertainty early enough for the owner, manager, contractor, and insurer to address it explicitly.

Check the boundaries before comparing totals

Start by marking every affected room, exterior area, unit, common space, and building system. For each location, ask which materials are inspected, protected, removed, cleaned, dried, tested, repaired, or excluded. Confirm whether the estimate covers only emergency mitigation or also remediation, contents work, and reconstruction. Similar headings can conceal very different deliverables; “water work,” for example, might mean extraction alone in one document and removal, equipment, monitoring, and disposal in another. Ask what conditions the estimator observed and which remain inaccessible or unknown. Assumptions should identify suspected source status, authorized access, available power, occupied areas, hazardous-material screening, and the condition of concealed cavities. Never treat an estimate assumption as a finding about cause, contamination, asbestos, structural stability, or safe occupancy. Those matters may require a qualified specialist or authority. List responsibilities beside the scope. Identify who moves contents, disconnects appliances, provides temporary security, coordinates tenants, obtains permits, orders inspections, retains engineers or hygienists, stores salvageable property, and disposes of material. Clarify whether subcontracted plumbing, electrical, HVAC, abatement, or structural services are included. A named coordinator is useful, but the owner should still know which party contracts with and supervises each specialist. Finally, define exclusions in plain language. Common uncertainties include finishes, matching, hidden damage, code-related changes, after-hours access, parking, elevator protection, storage duration, specialized cleaning, taxes, and permit fees. An exclusion is not necessarily improper; an invisible exclusion creates the risk.

Interrogate quantities, labour, equipment, and allowances

Request measurable units. Demolition may be expressed by area or length; equipment by type and duration; labour by task and hours; disposal by load, weight, or allowance; storage by container or period. Match quantities to a sketch, room list, or photo set. If two bids use different units, ask each provider to explain the conversion rather than making an unsupported comparison. Equipment entries should state purpose, count, charging basis, anticipated monitoring, and the event that ends the charge. Do not prescribe drying targets unless qualified to do so. Instead, ask who establishes the criteria, what records will show progress, and how equipment additions or removals are approved. The same discipline applies to air cleaning, containment, negative pressure, heating, and specialty devices: their presence does not itself prove a condition or clearance result. Separate fixed selections from allowances. An allowance reserves money for an item not fully selected or quantified; it is not a guaranteed final price. Record the allowance amount, assumed quantity and quality, selection deadline, markup treatment, and reconciliation method. For reconstruction, compare substrate repair, finish grade, installation, preparation, waste factors, and matching limitations. Ask how labour categories are defined and whether supervision, travel, documentation, protection, setup, cleaning, and project management appear separately or inside another rate. The goal is not to challenge every line mechanically. It is to ensure that apparent savings are not merely work shifted to the owner or omitted until later.

Control authorization and newly discovered work

Read the work authorization, direction-to-pay language, cancellation terms, payment schedule, interest terms, access permission, privacy clauses, and responsibility for uninsured amounts before signing. A direction to pay may affect how funds move, but it does not convert the contractor into the coverage decision-maker. Ask the insurer and an appropriate adviser about any language you do not understand. Create a change-order rule before opening walls or starting reconstruction. The rule should require a description of the new condition, photographs where safe, proposed response, cost and schedule effect, alternatives, and the person authorized to approve. Identify true emergency exceptions and how they will be documented afterward. Verbal urgency should not become a routine substitute for written control. Build a comparison sheet with columns for scope area, quantity, unit, unit cost, allowance, exclusion, responsible party, evidence required, and question status. Use “not stated” rather than guessing. Resolve material differences through written clarifications, then attach those clarifications to the signed agreement. Keep insurer estimates, contractor proposals, and owner upgrade choices distinguishable. They may use different assumptions and serve different purposes. Before final payment, reconcile approved changes, credits, deposits, taxes, contents charges, storage, and deficiencies. Collect lien-related or closeout documentation appropriate to the project with professional advice where necessary. This checklist improves visibility; it cannot promise price, schedule, insurance reimbursement, or a particular repair result.

Questions this guide answers

Why can two recovery estimates have very different totals?

They may use different boundaries, quantities, assumptions, equipment periods, allowances, exclusions, or responsibility assignments. Compare those elements before comparing totals.

What should a change order show?

It should identify the discovered condition, proposed work, evidence, alternatives, cost and timing effects, and authorized decision-maker.

Is an allowance a guaranteed final cost?

No. It is a provisional amount whose quantity, quality assumption, markup, selection deadline, and reconciliation method should be documented.
