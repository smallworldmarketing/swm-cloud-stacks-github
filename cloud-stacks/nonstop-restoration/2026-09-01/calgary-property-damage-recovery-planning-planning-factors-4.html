Calgary property damage recovery planning: Planning Factors 3

A question-led method for assigning authority, dependencies, records, and decision owners throughout a Calgary property-recovery project.

A workable recovery plan is more than a list of damaged rooms. It identifies who may authorize access, which hazards stop ordinary property work, what evidence must be preserved, and which decisions depend on specialists or public authorities. Begin with life safety: do not enter a restricted or visibly unsafe building, operate affected utilities, or disturb suspect hazardous material. Fire, gas, electrical, structural, sewage, chemical, trauma, and biohazard concerns require the appropriate authority or qualified professional before routine documentation or cleanup. Guidance should be checked against its current version; the public sources cited here were reviewed on September 1, 2026. The questions below help Calgary owners, tenants, and managers turn incomplete information into an accountable plan without guessing about coverage, clearance, cause, cost, or occupancy.

Which facts control the first plan?

Ask first: who currently controls access, and has that person or authority communicated restrictions in writing? The answer might involve an emergency authority, owner, landlord, property manager, insurer representative, or qualified specialist, but possession of a key does not by itself establish permission or safety. Record the name, role, time, instructions, and any restricted zones. If access is not confirmed, arrange remote exchanges of existing photographs, floor plans, lease records, maintenance files, and contact details instead of entering. Next ask what is known, what is merely reported, and what remains unknown. Separate direct observations—such as water visible from an authorized safe location—from interpretations about its origin or contamination. Do not label a cause of loss, diagnose mould, characterize asbestos, or declare a space safe. Create an uncertainty register with one owner and one next action for every open item. Examples include obtaining an electrician’s direction about an affected system, asking a structural professional about movement, or requesting a hazardous-material assessment before planned disturbance. Then identify the property relationships that affect authorization. A tenant may report damage but lack authority to commission building repairs. A condominium or multi-unit event may cross boundaries between a unit, common property, and neighbouring premises. A commercial manager may need owner approval before accepting a scope. Record who can authorize emergency measures, contents handling, destructive investigation, remediation, and reconstruction separately; one signature should not be assumed to cover every phase. Finally, define the information deadline rather than promising a completion date. Ask when the insurer, authority, specialist, occupant, and contractor need the next verified record. This produces a sequence based on dependencies instead of pressure. Alberta’s disaster guidance recommends documenting damage and contacting an insurer, while Calgary’s recovery information emphasizes following official direction. Neither source decides an individual policy or project outcome.

Who owns each decision and record?

Build a responsibility map with five columns: decision, authorized decision-maker, adviser, required evidence, and communication recipient. Use it for access, temporary security, property protection, sampling or assessment, contents movement, demolition, drying, specialist work, reconstruction, inspection, and re-occupancy. A restoration contractor may photograph conditions and describe proposed work, but cannot determine policy coverage, liability, insurer approval, settlement, or whether authorities consider a building occupiable. For every proposed activity, ask what must happen before it starts. Board-up may require access approval and a plan that preserves emergency egress. Opening an assembly may depend on hazardous-material review. Rebuilding may depend on permit discovery, an accepted written scope, and completed remediation evidence. Returning stored contents may depend on secure space, disposition decisions, and confirmation from the responsible professionals. Showing these links prevents one trade’s availability from becoming the project’s governing logic. Assign custody for records as deliberately as responsibility for work. Keep original photographs, video, inventories, incident notes, work authorizations, estimates, change orders, daily logs, measurements, invoices, specialist reports, permits, inspection results, warranties, and correspondence in a permanent loss file. Preserve original filenames and capture dates; use working copies for annotations. Log who supplied each document and whether it is draft, accepted, superseded, or final. Avoid editing an original image to highlight damage because the unaltered file may be important later. Before approving a plan, test it with four questions. Does every hazardous uncertainty lead to a stop or specialist decision? Does every activity have an authorized owner? Can a reader distinguish facts from assumptions? Can the next person reconstruct why a decision was made? If any answer is no, revise the map. The goal is not perfect prediction. It is a controlled process in which uncertainty remains visible and decisions can be traced.

Questions this guide answers

Does having a key mean someone may enter a damaged building?

No. Access depends on restrictions and direction from the person, authority, or qualified professional controlling entry; a key is not a safety determination.

Can a restoration contractor decide whether insurance covers proposed work?

No. A contractor can document conditions and work, but coverage, liability, approval, settlement, and claim outcomes belong to the insurer and other responsible parties.
