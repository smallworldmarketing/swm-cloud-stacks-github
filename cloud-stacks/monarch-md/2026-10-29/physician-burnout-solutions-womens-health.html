Physician Burnout Solutions for OB/GYNs: Evaluating Practice Model Change

Assess whether practice redesign addresses the actual sources of physician strain, with attention to workload, autonomy, staffing, and recovery time.

An OB/GYN physician considering a different practice model may be seeking a more sustainable working life. That goal deserves a careful assessment of the work itself. A change in payment structure, panel size, or ownership does not establish that the underlying sources of strain will improve. This article offers an organizational planning framework for discussing practice redesign. It does not diagnose burnout or promise that concierge medicine is a treatment. Physicians who need personal health support should obtain appropriate care; operational planning is a separate task that can examine the conditions around their work.

Identify the specific sources of strain

Describe difficult parts of the current week in observable terms. Examples might include unfinished documentation, repeated interruptions, unpredictable after-hours work, staffing gaps, or administrative tasks with no clear owner. Distinguish a problem's frequency from its emotional weight: an occasional event can still dominate a physician's experience. Collect staff perspectives as well as the physician's own account. Avoid reducing the assessment to appointment volume alone. The useful output is a short list of work conditions that need to change, with enough detail to compare alternative solutions. This makes the desired improvement more concrete than simply wanting a smaller practice.

Consider organizational changes before assuming conversion

NIOSH's Impact Wellbeing resources focus on workplace policies, practices, and systems. Although developed for healthcare organizations and hospital leaders, that systems perspective can inform questions about a smaller practice. Ask which difficulties could be addressed through staffing, routing, scheduling, or clearer responsibilities within the current model. Then compare those options with a more extensive redesign. A payment-model change may create opportunities, but it may also introduce unfamiliar work. Keep both possibilities visible. The assessment should explain why the proposed change addresses identified problems rather than relying on the general appeal of a different practice label.

Examine the entire workload, including hidden tasks

Map the work before, during, and after appointments. Include inbox review, coordination, staff questions, management decisions, and preparation for future visits. Ask which tasks would remain, move to another person, or newly appear in the proposed model. Test the allocation during a staff absence and after a period away from the office. A nominally lighter schedule can still leave unbounded work outside scheduled hours. Avoid replacing one undefined obligation with another, such as an unclear promise of constant availability. Review the proposed duties with the actual people expected to carry them and identify missing backup arrangements.

Translate autonomy into explicit decision rights

If greater control is a goal, specify the decisions the physician wants to influence: appointment length, staffing, service scope, communication rules, or time away. Ask how those decisions would be made under the proposed ownership and support arrangements. Consider which management responsibilities the physician wants and which would be delegated. Greater responsibility can be welcome, burdensome, or both, depending on the person and resources available. Discuss disagreements and escalation with prospective partners. The practical question is whether the arrangement provides meaningful control over the conditions that matter while supplying enough support to operate the practice.

Protect recovery time in the operating design

Review how the practice handles absence, evenings, weekends, and unexpected demand. Define responsibilities and routes for coverage instead of relying on informal goodwill. NIOSH's professional wellbeing materials discuss supportive leadership and attention to working conditions; they do not prescribe a concierge panel size or guarantee an outcome. Use the planned calendar to test whether the physician and staff can take time away without accumulating an unmanageable backlog. Include the work of covering for others in that test. A sustainable arrangement should be understandable to the team and reflected in patient communication, scheduling, and escalation procedures.

Evaluate progress with a reviewable hypothesis

Write a short statement connecting each proposed change to the problem it is intended to address. For example, a new routing process may aim to reduce avoidable interruptions, while protected administrative time may aim to reduce unfinished work. Choose practical observations to review after implementation and agree on a review date. Include staff feedback and unintended consequences rather than measuring only the desired result. If a change does not help, revise the process instead of treating the chosen practice model as beyond question. Sustainable work is an operating objective that requires continued attention, not an automatic consequence of conversion.
