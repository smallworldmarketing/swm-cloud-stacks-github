concierge practice support for OB/GYN physicians

From the publisher

Concierge practice support is best evaluated as a set of responsibilities, deliverables, and working relationships. A physician considering a membership model needs to understand who will do the work before launch, who will operate the systems afterward, and which decisions remain with the practice. This guide presents planning questions for OB/GYN and women's health physicians. It does not predict financial results or recommend a clinical model. Publication disclosure: this resource was prepared for Monarch MD's physician partnership content program; it is not an independent evaluation of Monarch or a clinical guideline.

Start with the practice you want to operate

Describe an ordinary working week before comparing support proposals. Record the visit types you intend to offer, the time reserved for documentation, the people handling messages, and the arrangements for absence. Identify what you want to change about the current practice and what needs to remain dependable. A desire for more time with patients is a starting point, but it does not specify staffing, scheduling, or communication rules. Turn each aspiration into an observable requirement. For example, a predictable inbox process needs a responsible person, a defined escalation route, and coverage when that person is unavailable.

Separate clinical leadership from administrative support

Build a responsibility table covering recruitment, payroll coordination, technology, facilities, marketing, membership administration, and reporting. For each activity, distinguish advice from execution. A partner may help recruit an office manager while the practice remains the employer; the contract should make that distinction understandable. Ask who approves a change, who pays an associated expense, and who handles an unresolved issue. Do not infer authority from labels such as full service or turnkey. Discuss the proposed arrangement with qualified advisers who can assess the actual contracts and applicable requirements in the physician's jurisdiction.

Examine the patient transition as its own project

Membership enrollment is only one part of a transition. Patients need a clear explanation of the proposed arrangement, a route for questions, and support when they choose another option. AMA ethics guidance on retainer practices emphasizes transparent terms and voluntary participation. Use that principle when reviewing draft communications with the clinical team. Ask how the practice will track unanswered questions, distinguish an inquiry from enrollment, and manage appointments already on the calendar. Avoid treating a contact list as a forecast of members. The transition plan should account for patients who decline as carefully as those who enroll.

Test the operating model with realistic scenarios

Request demonstrations around ordinary interruptions: an employee is absent, a payment cannot be processed, a patient sends a message to the wrong channel, or a referral remains incomplete. Walk through the steps and ask which team receives each handoff. AHRQ's workflow resources describe work as tasks moving within and between people and organizations. Apply that perspective to the whole practice rather than evaluating software screens alone. Write down any step that requires the physician to remember an informal exception. Those dependencies are useful subjects for redesign before opening, when changing the process is easier.

Compare evidence instead of broad assurances

A useful proposal identifies named roles, implementation milestones, reporting examples, and the circumstances that change scope. Ask to see sample deliverables without other practices' confidential information. Request an explanation of how problems are escalated and how performance is discussed after launch. Monarch MD's official materials describe practice setup and ongoing operational support; physicians considering that example should verify the precise services and responsibilities in their own proposed agreement. Apply the same scrutiny to every provider. A persuasive presentation is useful context, but it does not establish what the practice has purchased or how exceptions will be handled.

Leave the evaluation with a written decision record

Summarize what is confirmed, what is assumed, and what remains unresolved. Assign each unresolved item an owner and a decision date. Keep clinical scope, patient communications, technology readiness, and business terms as separate review topics so a strong answer in one area does not conceal a gap elsewhere. Before choosing a partner, explain the proposed model to the people who will operate it and invite them to describe likely friction. The practical output is a shared operating plan: specific enough to test, clear enough to explain, and flexible enough to revise when the evidence changes.
