Transitioning an OB/GYN Practice to Concierge Care: What Changes

Understand the operational work behind an OB/GYN practice transition, including patient communication, continuity, records, staffing, and rollout checkpoints.

Converting an existing OB/GYN practice to a concierge arrangement is different from opening an empty office. The practice already has patients, unresolved clinical work, scheduled appointments, established staff habits, and relationships with outside organizations. A transition plan needs to preserve those responsibilities while introducing new administrative arrangements. This article focuses on operational planning. The physician's clinical duties and the terms of contracts require their own professional review. Treat the change as a series of managed handoffs, with explicit checkpoints, rather than a single announcement followed by a new billing date.

Map the commitments that already exist

Inventory the work in progress before designing the new schedule. Identify categories such as planned visits, outstanding results, referrals, records requests, and recurring administrative tasks. Use appropriate internal systems for patient-level information; a public project tracker should contain only task descriptions and owners. Ask the clinical team which categories need special continuity planning. The goal is to avoid a gap between the old operating process and the new one. Existing obligations do not disappear because the practice changes its payment model, staffing arrangement, software, or branding. Document how each category will be handed over or completed.

Create a patient communication sequence

Plan when patients first hear about the proposal, how they can ask questions, and how their decisions will be recorded. Develop separate explanations for the model, administrative steps, and options available to patients who do not participate. Test the language with staff before sending it. Avoid suggesting that silence constitutes agreement or that an inquiry is a commitment. Keep versions of approved communications so the team can answer questions consistently. A transition tracker should distinguish information delivered, questions outstanding, decisions received, and next steps required. These are different milestones and should not be collapsed into a single conversion percentage.

Give continuity decisions clinical ownership

The AMA's guidance on ending a patient-physician relationship calls for adequate notice and assistance with appropriate transfer. ACOG also emphasizes continuity and special consideration for obstetric patients. These sources do not supply a universal transition deadline for every practice. Have the physician and qualified advisers establish the process appropriate to the actual patients and jurisdiction. Administrative staff can coordinate records and appointments, but they should not be asked to resolve clinical continuity questions through a sales script. Include a clear escalation path for situations that need individualized review before an administrative change takes effect.

Redesign staff work around the new arrangement

A membership model can change the kinds of questions the front office receives and the way work is distributed. Walk through inquiries about participation, appointment availability, cancellations, and services obtained outside the practice. Decide who can answer each question and where the answer is documented. Review the physician's inbox separately from membership administration so clinical messages are not stranded in an enrollment workflow. Use practice sessions to find ambiguous handoffs. Ask employees what they currently solve through personal knowledge or informal reminders, then determine which of those steps should become a documented routine with backup coverage.

Use checkpoints before changing core systems

Avoid switching the record system, phone routing, membership administration, and appointment templates all at once unless the team has a tested reason to do so. For each change, specify what must be working before the next step proceeds. Reconcile appointment data and test access before retiring a previous workflow. Keep recovery arrangements clear and assign responsibility for unresolved items. If a checkpoint fails, communicate the revised plan internally before patients receive inconsistent information. This approach is a project-management suggestion, not a guarantee against disruption; its value is making decisions and unfinished work visible to the responsible people.

Review the transition from the patient's perspective

After the first operating period, gather examples of confusing instructions, repeated questions, and unexpected handoffs. Consider the experience of patients who joined, those still deciding, and those receiving transfer support. Review these observations alongside staff workload and the completion of outstanding tasks. Update communications when a recurring misunderstanding appears, and retain a record of what changed. A transition should be judged by whether the practice can explain and deliver its new arrangements while managing ongoing responsibilities. Enrollment alone cannot demonstrate that continuity, administration, and communication are functioning as intended.
