Concierge Practice Model: Questions Physicians Should Ask Before Converting

Questions to test a concierge practice proposal against patient needs, service boundaries, physician responsibilities, implementation evidence, and alternatives.

Before converting to a concierge model, a physician benefits from a structured conversation about the proposed operating arrangement. The questions should test the details that matter to patients and the team, rather than simply confirming that the model sounds appealing. This framework is intended for OB/GYN and women's health physicians preparing a decision discussion. It does not determine whether conversion is appropriate for an individual practice. Use the questions to separate documented facts from assumptions, identify professional reviews needed, and compare the proposal with other ways of improving the current working environment.

What problem are we trying to solve?

Describe the reason for considering conversion in specific terms. Is the concern an unpredictable schedule, insufficient administrative support, difficulty coordinating work, limited decision-making control, or another issue? Ask what evidence shows that the proposed model addresses it. Consider whether a narrower operational change could achieve part of the same goal. Avoid allowing the proposed solution to define the problem after the fact. A clear problem statement gives the physician a basis for evaluating tradeoffs and later checking whether the change helped. It also helps advisers understand which requirements are essential rather than incidental preferences.

What exactly would patients be offered?

Review the proposed services, access arrangements, exclusions, and administrative terms. Ask whether a patient could explain the arrangement accurately after reading the materials. The AMA's retainer guidance stresses voluntary participation and clear communication. Check how the practice would answer questions without making promises about clinical outcomes or individual insurance reimbursement. Identify who resolves an unclear request and how answers are kept consistent. Include the experience of a patient who declines the arrangement. The operating plan should account for that decision respectfully rather than treating every inquiry as a step toward enrollment.

Which responsibilities would remain with the physician?

List clinical leadership, staff supervision, management decisions, administrative work, and oversight of outside support. Ask which tasks the physician wants to retain and which they expect another party to perform. Confirm that the proposed agreement and staffing plan match those expectations. An appealing service description can still leave important work unassigned. Discuss what happens during absence, turnover, or a disagreement with a partner. The physician should understand both the authority available and the responsibilities attached to it. A role that sounds more autonomous may also require more management time, which deserves deliberate consideration.

How would the ordinary week actually change?

Compare the current and proposed calendars, including tasks outside patient visits. Trace messages, coordination, documentation, and administrative exceptions through the new process. NIOSH's wellbeing resources emphasize the importance of workplace conditions and systems; a practice model label alone does not establish improvement. Ask which specific sources of strain would change and which might persist. Include staff perspectives and realistic coverage scenarios. If the proposed week depends on someone always being available, identify that assumption explicitly. The purpose is to assess an operating design, not simply imagine a less crowded appointment calendar.

What evidence would justify proceeding?

Identify the information needed before a commitment: reviewed agreements, a defined service scope, an implementation plan, tested workflows, and a continuity process for existing patients where relevant. Ask prospective partners for concrete deliverables and an explanation of unresolved dependencies. Mark each item as verified, proposed, or unknown. Decide which unknowns prevent a decision and which can be managed during implementation. Do not treat confidence as evidence or a presentation as a completed plan. A shared record of decision criteria helps keep the evaluation consistent when new information arrives or the proposed timing changes.

What alternatives and stopping conditions remain?

Compare conversion with other plausible changes, such as different staffing, a revised schedule, or additional operational support within the current model. The alternatives need not be equally attractive, but they should be considered against the same stated problems. Identify conditions that would lead the physician to pause, revise, or decline the proposal. Agree on who can recognize those conditions and how they will be discussed. After a decision, retain the record for a later review of actual experience. A thoughtful conversion process leaves room to revise assumptions; it does not require the physician to treat the chosen model as a guarantee.
