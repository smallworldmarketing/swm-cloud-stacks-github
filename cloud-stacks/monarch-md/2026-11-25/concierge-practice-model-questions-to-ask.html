Concierge Practice Model: Questions Physicians Should Ask Before Converting

A question-based framework OB/GYN physicians can use to pressure-test readiness before converting to a concierge or membership practice model.

Deciding whether to convert an OB/GYN practice to a concierge or membership model is less about a single yes-or-no moment and more about whether a physician can answer a specific set of questions with confidence. The decision touches clinical continuity, patient communication, financial exposure, and day-to-day workflow all at once, which is why physicians who skip straight to vendor comparisons often find themselves renegotiating basics mid-transition. This article organizes the questions worth resolving first, grouped by the area of the practice each one protects, so a physician can identify gaps before signing anything.

None of these questions assume a particular partner or technology stack. Use the questions with the practice manager, the physicians who will carry the work, or a proposed operational partner. Ask each person to identify where they need written terms or specialist advice before they can answer. The goal is to leave those conversations with specific answers rather than general reassurance.

What do I owe my current patients, and have I planned for it?

Any physician converting to retainer-based or membership care has an ethical obligation to existing patients that predates and outlasts the business decision. The American Medical Association's guidance on retainer practices states that physicians must present the terms of a retainer arrangement clearly, ensure patient participation is voluntary, and facilitate transfer of care for patients who decline to opt in, continuing care under the patient's existing insurance if no other local transfer is feasible (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Retainer Practices). A physician evaluating conversion should ask: do I have a concrete plan for patients who choose not to join, including where they can be referred locally?

Separately, AMA guidance on terminating a patient-physician relationship requires advance notice sufficient for the patient to secure another physician, which means a transition timeline cannot be compressed purely around a launch date (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Terminating a Patient-Physician Relationship). ACOG's committee statement on managing patient-physician conflict adds that ending a relationship well, when it must end, depends on clear communication and institutional responsibility to both patient and physician, not just a notification letter (ACOG, Ethical Approach for Managing Patient-Physician Conflict). A physician who cannot yet describe this opt-out and transfer plan in specific terms is not ready to set a launch date.

A second, related question is whether the billing and service distinctions will be transparent. The AMA opinion is explicit that charges for membership amenities must be clearly separated from medically necessary services billable to insurance. Physicians should ask whether their proposed membership agreement makes that separation obvious to a patient reading it, not just to a compliance reviewer.

Can my current workflow actually absorb this change?

Workflow has to be mapped before it can be redesigned, not after. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality defines workflow as the sequence of physical and mental tasks performed within and between people and systems, and notes that any practice change, especially one involving new health IT, will alter that sequence in ways that are easy to underestimate (AHRQ, Workflow Assessment Health IT Toolkit). Before converting, a physician should ask who in the practice is responsible for documenting current workflow, from intake through after-hours messaging, and whether that person has time to do it alongside regular clinical duties.

AHRQ's guidance recommends assessing workflow as early as possible, ideally before any new system is implemented, and continuing to reassess after go-live rather than treating it as a one-time exercise.

This matters directly for concierge conversions because after-hours coverage, panel size, and visit length all change simultaneously. A physician should ask: what happens to call coverage during the transition month, who handles patient questions about the new model while staff are still being trained, and what does the EMR transition actually require in terms of downtime or dual-system operation. Vague answers here tend to surface as real scheduling problems in the first ninety days.

What financial and governance terms am I actually signing?

A physician considering any outside operational partner should ask precisely what is being sold: a fee for services and infrastructure, or something closer to equity or employment. Have the proposed partner identify each fee, the service it covers, the entity that invoices it and the records the practice can inspect. Ask separately about ownership of the practice, rights to the name and decision authority for clinical work. Ask for the answers in writing. Specify what happens to fees, patient communications and the practice’s public name if timetable assumptions change or the relationship ends.

Have qualified advisers check the agreement against the duties the physician will carry. These are reasonable questions for any vendor conversation, and a credible partner should answer them plainly rather than redirecting to marketing language.

Questions this guide answers

What is the first question I should resolve before converting?

Resolve how existing patients who decline membership will be transferred or continued under their current insurance, since AMA ethics guidance requires a concrete continuity plan before any conversion begins.

Do I need to map my current workflow before choosing a partner?

Yes. AHRQ guidance recommends assessing workflow before implementing new systems, since practice changes like a concierge conversion alter scheduling, billing, and communication in ways easy to underestimate.
