Independent Physician Practice Checklist Before Going Concierge

A readiness checklist of questions OB/GYNs should resolve about ownership, staffing, and patient transition before converting to a concierge model.

Deciding whether a practice is actually ready to convert to a concierge or membership model is a different question than deciding whether the model itself appeals to you. Readiness is about specific, answerable facts: who holds decision-making authority today, what the current patient panel looks like, what workflow changes a new model would trigger, and what obligations exist to patients who may not follow you into the new structure. This checklist is built around those questions rather than around marketing claims from any vendor.

The goal is to give a physician a structured way to self-assess before signing anything or committing to a timeline. Some answers will point toward readiness; others will surface gaps that need to close first, such as an incomplete picture of current patient volume or an unresolved question about who owns the medical records if the practice location changes. Working through the list in order, rather than skipping to the parts that feel comfortable, produces a more honest picture of where a practice actually stands.

Ownership, governance, and decision rights

Start by confirming who actually controls clinical decisions, pricing, and hiring in the current practice, and compare that to what would change under a concierge structure. A physician who has been an employee or junior partner in a larger group may be used to decisions being made elsewhere; independent ownership means those decisions, along with the liability and administrative load that come with them, sit with the physician directly.

Ask specifically how any proposed partner or support arrangement would be structured. The American Medical Association's guidance on retainer practices notes that physicians entering these arrangements must present terms clearly to patients and must not imply that better medical care comes with a retainer fee, since the fee covers access and amenities rather than superior clinical treatment (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 11.2.5). That distinction matters internally too: a practice converting to membership should be clear, in its own planning documents, about what the fee is actually purchasing before it tries to explain that to patients.

A second governance question concerns long-term control. If a support company or management partner is involved, what does its agreement say about equity, revenue share, and the physician's ability to make independent clinical calls? These terms vary by agreement and should be reviewed individually rather than assumed from general marketing language, since precise services and structures differ across proposals.

Patient panel and continuity obligations

Before converting, a physician needs a clear-eyed count of the current active patient panel and a realistic estimate of how many patients are likely to enroll in a membership model versus seek care elsewhere. This is a planning exercise, not a guess made under deadline pressure, and it directly affects staffing and cash-flow timing during the transition window.

Continuity of care for patients who do not join the new model is an ethical obligation, not a logistical afterthought. AMA guidance on retainer practices specifically requires physicians to facilitate transfer of care for patients who decline to participate, and if a feasible transfer isn't available, to continue care under the patient's existing insurance until other arrangements can be made (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 11.2.5). Separately, the AMA's opinion on terminating a patient-physician relationship requires advance notice sufficient for the patient to secure another physician and requires facilitating that transfer (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 1.1.5).

ACOG's 2022 committee statement on managing patient-physician conflict and ending the relationship adds a related expectation: physicians should first attempt to resolve strain in the relationship where possible, and when ending care is the right outcome, should follow an ethical framework that accounts for institutional responsibilities to both patient and physician (ACOG Committee Statement No. 3, December 2022). A readiness checklist should include a concrete plan for identifying which patients will need transfer assistance and a timeline for communicating with them, not just a plan for enrolling members.

Workflow, staffing, and systems readiness

Converting a practice model changes daily workflow in ways that are easy to underestimate from the outside. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's guidance on health IT workflow assessment describes workflow as the sequence of physical and mental tasks performed within and between people and organizations, and warns that changes to practice management processes, including technology changes, routinely create unanticipated effects on patient care and billing if workflow isn't assessed in advance (AHRQ Workflow Assessment Toolkit).

Applied to a concierge conversion, this means mapping out exactly how scheduling, message triage, after-hours coverage, and billing will function under the new model before opening day, not learning the gaps in real time. Questions worth resolving include: who answers patient calls outside clinic hours, how prescription refills and referrals will be routed, and whether the current EMR supports the membership billing and documentation the new model requires.

Staffing is the other half of this. An independent concierge practice still needs an office manager and clinical support staff, and a physician should have a realistic plan for hiring, training, and retaining that team rather than assuming a partner organization will absorb all of it. Where outside support is part of the plan, the checklist should include confirming exactly which functions the partner performs versus which remain the physician's direct responsibility, since this varies by agreement and should be reviewed line by line rather than assumed.

Questions this guide answers

What should a physician confirm about patient continuity before converting to concierge care?

A physician should confirm there is a concrete plan to transfer or continue care for patients who choose not to join the membership model. AMA guidance on retainer practices requires facilitating transfer of care for patients who opt out, and continuing care under their existing insurance if no transfer is immediately feasible. This obligation exists independently of how the new practice is structured or who provides operational support.

Why does workflow assessment matter before a practice model change?

Workflow assessment matters because practice management processes, including scheduling, billing, and patient communication, change substantially when a practice converts models, and AHRQ's guidance notes that skipping this assessment tends to cause delays in care and billing. Mapping workflow in advance, rather than after launch, reduces the chance of gaps appearing during the busiest transition period.

Does working with an outside support company mean giving up practice ownership?

Not necessarily, but this depends entirely on the terms of the specific agreement, so it should never be assumed. Physicians evaluating any partnership should review exactly what equity, governance, and clinical decision rights the agreement assigns, since these terms vary and require individual review rather than reliance on general descriptions.
