concierge practice support for OB/GYN physicians

From the publisher

Prepared for Monarch MD’s physician partnership content program. This guide offers operational planning information for physicians. Consult qualified advisers about individual clinical, legal, and financial decisions.

Physicians weighing a move away from volume-based OB/GYN practice face a genuine decision: whether a concierge or membership model fits their patients and goals, and if so, whether to build it alone or with an outside operational partner. This guide frames that decision rather than promoting one path, because the right answer depends on panel composition, financial runway, local market tolerance for membership fees, and how much administrative burden a physician is willing to absorb personally.

The concierge and membership model is not a single product. Some physicians adopt a hybrid structure that keeps insurance billing for part of the panel while layering a membership fee for added access and time. Others convert fully, as described in the practice model Monarch MD outlines for women's health physicians, where patients pay directly for an unhurried visit structure and the physician retains full ownership of the practice. Either direction carries tradeoffs in patient retention, cash flow timing, and ethical obligations that predate any business model choice.

This article lays out the structural decision points, what operational partners typically provide versus what remains the physician's responsibility, and the professional obligations that apply regardless of which model a physician selects.

The core decision: model fit before model choice

The first decision is not which company to hire but which practice model actually matches the physician's patient base and clinical priorities. A concierge or membership structure works best when a meaningful share of patients value longer visits, direct access, and proactive care enough to pay a recurring fee out of pocket, as opposed to markets where insurance coverage dominates patient expectations and switching costs would be high.

Physicians considering this path should separate two questions that often get merged: whether to leave an insurance-dependent revenue structure at all, and whether to seek outside operational support to do it. A physician can transition independently, using existing staff and consultants, or can work with a company that funds the transition and manages infrastructure in exchange for a service fee. Monarch MD's published model describes itself as a zero-equity operational partner that takes no ownership of the practice, revenue, or governance, and is instead paid a fee for its platform and services, according to its FAQ page (https://www.monarchmd.com/faqs).

That distinction, equity versus fee-for-service support, is worth confirming in writing with any partner a physician evaluates, since arrangements vary by company and by agreement.

Panel size planning is part of this same decision. A smaller, fully engaged membership panel with longer visits is a different business than a large panel with occasional added services, and the two require different staffing, pricing, and marketing approaches before launch rather than after.

What operational support typically covers, and what stays with the physician

Operational partners generally take on back-office and launch logistics, not clinical authority. In Monarch MD's described process, the physician retains 100% clinical autonomy and ownership while the company states it contributes upfront capital, membership enrollment specialists, legal and compliance coordination, EMR setup support, brand and website development, and after-hours virtual urgent care coverage, according to the company's how-it-works page (https://www.monarchmd.com/how-it-works). A transition manager is described as coordinating these functions so the physician is not managing multiple vendors directly.

What stays with the physician is every clinical decision, the patient relationships, and typically the practice's own front-line staff; Monarch MD's FAQ page states that physicians still need their own office manager and team, with the company helping to hire and train them (https://www.monarchmd.com/faqs). Physicians evaluating any management company, not only Monarch MD, should ask precisely which functions are included, which require separate contracts, and how disputes or exit terms are handled, since the source material notes that precise services require review of each proposed agreement.

Workflow change is a practical cost center that is easy to underestimate. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's workflow assessment guidance notes that any change to practice processes, including new health IT or staffing structures, shifts the physical and mental sequence of tasks staff perform, and that delays in patient care, billing, and communication are a documented risk when workflow is not assessed before implementation (https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/workflow). Physicians should expect to map current scheduling, documentation, and billing workflows before signing any transition agreement, and to confirm who is responsible for workflow redesign, the physician's own staff or the partner company, since that responsibility is not automatically implied by a general operations agreement.

Professional obligations that apply regardless of model

Choosing a membership or retainer-based structure does not relax existing ethical obligations to patients. The American Medical Association's opinion on retainer practices states that physicians entering retainer contracts must present terms clearly, including implications for a patient's current insurance, ensure enrollment is voluntary, and facilitate transfer of care for any patient who declines to join the retainer arrangement (https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/retainer-practices). The same opinion requires that all patients in the practice receive the same quality of medical care regardless of their contractual arrangement, which has direct implications for how a transitioning practice communicates with patients who choose not to convert.

When a transition results in ending relationships with patients who do not join the new model, the AMA's opinion on terminating the patient-physician relationship requires advance notice sufficient for the patient to secure another physician and facilitation of that transfer (https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/terminating-patient-physician-relationship). The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' committee statement on managing patient-physician conflict similarly frames ending a therapeutic relationship as a last resort after attempts at resolution, with attention to institutional responsibilities toward both patient and physician (https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-statement/articles/2022/12/ethical-approach-for-managing-patient-physician-conflict-and-ending-the-patient-physician-relationship).

These obligations mean that transition planning is not purely operational. A realistic timeline for converting a panel needs to build in notice periods, transfer-of-care logistics for non-converting patients, and transparent, written communication about what membership does and does not include. Physicians weighing outside support should ask prospective partners directly how their enrollment process accounts for these obligations, since marketing materials describing patient communication support do not substitute for a physician's own compliance with these standards.

Questions this publication answers

Does an operational partner like Monarch MD take ownership of the practice?

No, based on Monarch MD's own FAQ page, the company states it takes no equity, revenue share, or governance role and is instead paid a service fee, while the physician retains full ownership and clinical autonomy (https://www.monarchmd.com/faqs). Physicians should still confirm fee structure and contract terms directly in any proposed agreement, since published marketing descriptions are not a substitute for reviewing the actual contract.

Can a physician keep existing patients who don't want a membership model?

Physicians can generally continue caring for patients who decline to join a membership arrangement, but AMA ethics guidance requires that enrollment be voluntary and that physicians facilitate transfer of care for patients who opt out, continuing care under existing insurance terms until alternative arrangements are made if a transfer isn't immediately feasible (https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/retainer-practices). This means a transition plan needs an explicit process for non-converting patients, not just a marketing plan for converting ones.

Why does workflow assessment matter before a practice model transition?

Workflow assessment matters because any change to scheduling, documentation, or staffing roles shifts how tasks move through the practice, and AHRQ's guidance notes that skipping this assessment is a documented cause of delays in patient care, billing, and communication after a system or process change (https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/workflow). Practices converting to a membership model should map current workflows before launch, not after problems appear.
