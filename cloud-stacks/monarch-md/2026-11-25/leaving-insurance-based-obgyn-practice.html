Leaving an Insurance-Based OB/GYN Practice: Questions to Resolve First

A worked decision scenario for OB/GYN physicians weighing an exit from insurance contracts, covering panel math, patient continuity, and payer timing questions.

Deciding whether to leave an insurance-based OB/GYN practice is less a single choice than a sequence of smaller decisions about money, patients, and timing that have to be answered in the right order. A physician cannot responsibly set a departure date until she knows how many current patients would realistically convert to a membership model, what obligations she owes departing patients, and how payer contracts and runout claims affect cash flow during the gap. This article walks through that sequence as a worked scenario rather than a general overview, because the order in which these questions get answered determines whether the transition is financially survivable and ethically sound.

Consider a physician whose current patients use several payers and have appointments booked under existing arrangements. She wants to understand what would change before she tells staff or patients that the practice will leave a network. The scenario below works through the financial, patient-continuity, and operational questions she would need to resolve before giving notice to any payer or publicly announcing a change in practice model.

How much of the current panel needs to convert to make the numbers work?

The first number to solve for is not total patients but converting patients, because a concierge or membership practice intentionally serves a much smaller panel at a higher price per patient. The practice can record the patients it currently sees, the appointments already scheduled and the questions patients have raised. Its advisers can then evaluate membership assumptions, payer arrangements and obligations around continuity before anyone treats an expression of interest as a decision.

Separate patient survey responses from signed agreements and record how and when each answer was obtained. Ask a qualified adviser to review the practice’s own information and identify the unresolved questions before comparing operating models. That sequencing detail matters regardless of which partner or model a physician considers: panel-specific numbers should exist before a transition date is set, not after. A physician working independently would need to build this estimate herself, using patient interest surveys or informal outreach, before committing to a departure timeline.

A second financial question in this scenario is runout: insurance claims for services already rendered continue to arrive for months after a physician stops accepting new patients under a payer contract. Budgeting for that runout period, alongside startup costs for a new model, is part of the same calculation as panel conversion, not a separate afterthought.

What does the physician owe patients who will not convert?

Patients who do not join a membership model are owed continuity of care, and that obligation shapes the timeline more than any financial projection does. The American Medical Association's guidance on terminating a patient-physician relationship states that physicians must notify patients far enough in advance to let them secure another physician and must facilitate transfer of care when appropriate (https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/terminating-patient-physician-relationship). In the worked scenario, this means the physician cannot simply stop seeing insurance patients on the date her membership practice opens; she needs a notice period and a plan for where non-converting patients, including those mid-pregnancy or managing an active gynecologic condition, will go next.

The AMA's opinion on retainer practices adds a related obligation: physicians who ask patients to pay for a membership arrangement must present the terms clearly, confirm that accepting the arrangement is voluntary, and facilitate transfer of care for anyone who declines (https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/retainer-practices). For an OB/GYN specifically, ACOG's committee statement on managing patient-physician conflict and ending the relationship describes an ethical framework for cases where the relationship cannot continue, including institutional responsibilities to both patient and physician during the transition (https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-statement/articles/2022/12/ethical-approach-for-managing-patient-physician-conflict-and-ending-the-patient-physician-relationship). Applied to this scenario, the physician should map her active obstetric patients separately from her annual-visit gynecology patients, since continuity risk is highest for anyone in an active course of care.

A practical follow-up question is where non-converting patients actually go. In markets with few OB/GYNs accepting new patients, the physician may need a longer notice window or a referral relationship with a colleague who still accepts the relevant insurance, which should be arranged before notice goes out, not during it.

What operational and payer-contract steps determine the exit date?

The exit date in this scenario is set by contract terms and workflow readiness, not by physician preference alone. Most payer contracts specify a notice period for terminating participation, and that window needs to be checked for each payer separately, since commercial and Medicaid contracts often carry different notice and claims-runout terms. The physician in this scenario would need to request her contract terms in writing before finalizing any public transition date, since giving notice prematurely can create a gap where she is contractually out-of-network but not yet operationally ready for a membership model.

Workflow readiness is the other half of the timing question. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's guidance on health IT workflow assessment notes that any change to practice management processes changes the underlying workflow, and that delays in patient care, billing, and communication are likely if that workflow is not assessed in advance (https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/workflow). Applied here, the physician needs her new scheduling system, billing process for membership fees, and after-hours coverage plan tested before the last insurance-based patient day, not improvised afterward.

A final question worth resolving before setting a date is staffing continuity: does current office staff understand the new billing model, and has someone been assigned to own patient communications during the notice period. Keep a separate record of who owns staff training, who drafts patient notices and who can approve the timing. Ask each person to identify the documents or decisions needed before the next step can begin. A physician working without that kind of coordinator still needs to assign those responsibilities explicitly before the notice period starts, so that patient questions during the transition are answered consistently.

Questions this guide answers

How many patients typically need to convert for the math to work?

There is no universal percentage, because the answer depends on membership pricing, service structure, and the physician's target panel size, which is why panel-specific financial analysis needs to happen before a departure date is set. A qualified practice adviser can review how participation, appointment capacity and commitments to existing patients affect the particular practice. The physician should bring the current agreements, scheduling records and the evidence behind each assumption. A physician planning independently should build the same kind of estimate using direct patient outreach before committing to an exit timeline.

Can a physician keep seeing some patients under insurance while building a membership panel?

Many physicians do not need to leave all insurance contracts at once, and some continue treating existing patients under their current arrangement while onboarding members separately, depending on payer contract terms and practical scheduling limits. This hybrid period is a timing and workflow question specific to each contract, so the notice and runout terms for each payer should be confirmed individually before assuming a hybrid approach is available.

What notice is a physician obligated to give patients before ending the relationship?

Physicians must notify patients far enough in advance for them to secure another physician and must facilitate transfer of care when appropriate, per the AMA's ethics opinion on terminating a patient-physician relationship (https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/terminating-patient-physician-relationship). The exact length of notice is not fixed by this guidance and may also be shaped by state regulations or payer contract terms, so physicians should confirm both before finalizing a timeline.
