Choosing a Physician Practice Management Company: A Decision Framework

A structured way for OB/GYN physicians to evaluate practice management partners before signing: ownership terms, workflow fit, and transition support.

Choosing a physician practice management company is a structural decision, not a vendor purchase, because the agreement shapes who controls clinical decisions, who owns the practice name, and how patient transitions are handled for years afterward. OB/GYN physicians considering concierge or membership models typically compare several companies that offer capital, staffing help, and back-office infrastructure in exchange for an ongoing fee. The decision is harder to reverse than most practice purchases, so it benefits from a framework rather than a feature checklist.

This article lays out the questions a physician should work through in order, moving from ownership structure to operational fit to patient-transition obligations. It does not recommend a specific company or predict financial outcomes, since those depend on the terms of each individual agreement and the physician's existing panel. It draws on AMA ethics guidance on retainer practices and on AHRQ's workflow assessment framework to ground the operational questions in sourced standards rather than marketing claims.

Step one: confirm what the company actually owns or controls

The first filter in any practice management evaluation is ownership and governance, because some arrangements involve equity stakes, employment relationships, or control over clinical decisions, while others are structured as fee-based services with no ownership claim. A physician should ask directly whether the company takes any share of the practice, its revenue, or its governance, and whether the physician remains the employer of record for practice staff. Ask the company to identify the legal entity that signs, list any decision rights it would receive, and show where the agreement gives the physician control.

Keep a written record of answers about hiring, billing, practice assets and access to records so a qualified adviser can compare them with the proposed terms. Whatever the structure, the physician's attorney should review the agreement line by line rather than relying on a summary, since the practical difference between 'partner' and 'owner' is defined by contract language, not by marketing copy.

A second governance question concerns the practice's identity: does the physician keep the practice name, brand, and website if the relationship ends? Some companies build the brand around the physician's own name and specialty; others attach the practice to a shared brand that the physician would lose on exit. This matters more in OB/GYN and women's health than in many specialties, because patient relationships often take years to build and a name change at exit can disrupt continuity of care, an obligation physicians carry under AMA guidance on retainer practices and on ending the patient-physician relationship (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 11.2.5; Opinion 1.1.5).

Step two: map the company's role against your actual daily workflow

Before comparing pricing or services, a physician should map current workflow bottlenecks and ask which specific tasks the management company will absorb versus which remain the physician's responsibility. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality frames workflow as the sequence of tasks performed by people within and across systems, and stresses that any change to a practice, including a change in management structure, will alter that sequence in ways that are easy to underestimate (AHRQ, Workflow Assessment Health IT Toolkit). Applied to a management-company decision, this means asking concretely: who schedules patients, who handles after-hours calls, who manages the EMR transition, and who trains new staff.

Vague language like 'full operational support' should be replaced with a specific list matched against the physician's current pain points.

It also helps to separate one-time launch tasks from ongoing operational support, since a company strong on the former (brand design, office build-out, initial enrollment) may offer thinner support once the practice is running. Physicians evaluating multiple companies that help OB/GYNs start a concierge practice should ask each one to describe its post-launch model: Does an office manager get hired and trained for the physician's own team, or does the company supply shared staff? Is after-hours coverage provided by the company's clinicians, and under what terms does that coverage end if the contract ends?

These workflow questions reveal more about day-to-day fit than a feature list does.

Step three: pressure-test the patient-transition plan against ethical obligations

Any practice management company involved in a concierge or membership conversion should have a concrete, physician-led plan for notifying existing patients, and that plan must satisfy the physician's independent ethical obligations, not just the company's enrollment targets. AMA guidance on retainer practices requires that patients be told the terms clearly, that acceptance be voluntary, and that physicians facilitate transfer of care for patients who decline to enroll, continuing care under existing insurance terms if no local transfer is feasible (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 11.2.5). ACOG's committee statement on managing patient-physician conflict adds that ending a therapeutic relationship should follow an ethical framework, with adequate notice and a genuine attempt at resolution when conflict arises during a transition (ACOG Committee Statement No. 3, December 2022).

A physician evaluating a management company should ask how the company's enrollment materials and membership specialists communicate with patients, whether the messaging is written in the physician's own voice, and who is responsible if a patient needs continued care on their existing insurance. These are not administrative details; they determine whether the practice transition can be defended as ethically sound if questioned later. A company's standard materials should be reviewed by the physician's own counsel alongside the broader agreement, since contract terms around patient communication can shift financial and legal exposure in ways that are not obvious from a sales conversation alone.

Questions this guide answers

What is the first thing to check before choosing a practice management company?

Confirm exactly what the company owns or controls before comparing anything else, since some arrangements involve equity or employment while others are fee-based services with no ownership claim. Ask directly whether the company takes any share of practice revenue or governance, and have the physician's own attorney review the agreement rather than relying on a company summary. This single question affects everything downstream, including what happens to the practice name if the relationship ends.

How do I compare workflow support between different companies?

List the physician's actual daily bottlenecks first, then ask each company to map its services against that specific list rather than a generic feature sheet. AHRQ's workflow framework notes that any operational change alters the sequence of tasks across people and systems, so vague claims of 'full support' should be replaced with specifics on scheduling, after-hours coverage, EMR setup, and staff training. Comparing companies against the same concrete workflow list makes differences easier to see than comparing marketing materials.

What ethical obligations apply when patients are transitioned to a new practice model?

Physicians must present retainer or membership terms clearly, ensure patient participation is voluntary, and facilitate transfer of care for patients who decline to enroll, continuing care under existing insurance terms if no local transfer is available. These obligations come from AMA guidance on retainer practices and apply regardless of which management company is involved. A management company's enrollment materials should be reviewed against these standards, since the ethical responsibility rests with the physician, not the vendor.
