Physician Burnout Solutions for OB/GYNs: Evaluating Practice Model Change

A worked scenario for OB/GYNs weighing whether burnout stems from workload, workflow design, or the practice model itself before changing structure.

An OB/GYN physician considering burnout solutions faces a diagnostic question before a structural one: is the exhaustion coming from visit volume, from broken clinic workflow, or from a payor-driven model that caps time regardless of effort? This article walks through a worked scenario rather than a generic list of burnout tips, because the right fix depends on which of these is actually driving the fatigue. Physicians who skip this diagnosis often adopt solutions, such as adding staff or software, that never touch the underlying cause.

The scenario below follows a composite OB/GYN physician evaluating whether a practice model change, rather than incremental fixes, is the appropriate response to sustained burnout. The goal is to give readers a framework for running this same analysis on their own schedule, payor mix, and daily workflow.

Separating workflow problems from model problems

A physician seeing 24 patients a day in nine-minute increments may assume the fix is a scribe or a better EHR template. Sometimes that is correct. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's workflow guidance defines workflow as the sequence of physical and mental tasks performed within and between people and systems, and it notes that delays in care, billing, and communication tend to appear specifically when workflow is overlooked or oversimplified during a change (AHRQ, Workflow Assessment Health IT Toolkit). That distinction matters here: a scheduling bottleneck, a documentation backlog, or a referral-routing problem is a workflow issue, and workflow issues are often fixable inside an existing practice model.

The harder case is when the visit length itself is the constraint. If a physician's payor contracts set the appointment length, no amount of staffing or software redesign can extend the minutes available for a complex perimenopausal consult or a new obstetric patient with multiple risk factors. In the worked scenario, the physician ran a two-week log of which encounters felt rushed and found that the rushed visits were not caused by inefficiency inside the visit; they were capped before the clinical conversation was finished. That is a model-level constraint, not a workflow defect, and it points toward evaluating membership-based or concierge structures rather than tweaking the existing schedule.

What a model change actually requires before committing

A practice model change is not a quick substitute for workflow fixes, and treating it that way creates new problems. Physicians who are considering a retainer or membership arrangement have a specific ethical obligation to examine first: the AMA's guidance on retainer practices requires that patients be told clearly what the arrangement changes, that participation remain voluntary, and that physicians facilitate transfer of care for any patient who does not want to join (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 11.2.5). This is not paperwork to delegate entirely; it shapes how a burnout-driven transition is communicated to an existing panel.

The worked scenario physician also had to confirm how the shift would affect active patients who chose not to convert. The AMA's opinion on terminating a patient-physician relationship requires advance notice sufficient for the patient to find another physician and requires facilitating that transfer when appropriate (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 1.1.5). ACOG's committee guidance on managing patient-physician conflict adds that ending a therapeutic relationship should follow an attempt at resolution and should include institutional responsibility to both patient and physician during the transition (ACOG Committee Statement No. 3, 2022).

None of this is a reason to avoid a model change for burnout reasons; it is the groundwork that determines whether the change is executed responsibly rather than abruptly.

The practical sequence that emerged from the scenario: log actual visit-length and task patterns for two to four weeks, separate what a longer appointment would fix from what better routing or staffing would fix, and only then evaluate whether membership-based structures, which typically allow 45- to 60-minute visits with smaller daily panels, address the root cause rather than its symptoms.

Questions this guide answers

How do I know if burnout is a workflow problem or a model problem?

Track which visits feel rushed and why. If the cause is appointment length set by payor contracts, it is a model constraint; if it is routing, documentation, or staffing delays, it is workflow and often fixable without restructuring.

Do I have to tell current patients before changing my practice model?

Yes. AMA ethics guidance on retainer practices requires clear disclosure of the arrangement's terms, voluntary participation, and facilitated transfer of care for patients who choose not to join.
