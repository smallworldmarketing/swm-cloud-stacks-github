Transitioning an OB/GYN Practice to Concierge Care: What Changes

A planning guide to what actually changes operationally, ethically, and financially when an OB/GYN practice converts to a concierge or membership model.

Converting an OB/GYN practice to a concierge or membership model is less a single decision than a sequence of operational changes that touch scheduling, staffing, billing, patient communication, and physician obligations to existing patients. The reader's task here is practical: understand which parts of a practice have to change, in what order, and what professional obligations govern the transition, so the plan can be evaluated against real workflow and ethical requirements rather than marketing language alone. This guide does not estimate revenue or recommend a specific vendor; it maps the mechanics physicians need to work through before signing any agreement.

The scope of change is often underestimated because concierge conversion is frequently described as a scheduling or pricing change. In practice, it restructures the patient relationship, the office's daily workflow, and the legal terms under which existing patients are treated. Physicians evaluating a transition, whether independently or with an operational partner, benefit from separating three distinct categories of change: what happens to the existing patient panel, what happens to daily clinical workflow, and what happens to staffing and back-office operations.

What changes for existing patients and the physician's ethical obligations

Existing patients cannot simply be reassigned to a new payment structure without formal notice and a path to alternative care. The American Medical Association's guidance on retainer practices states that physicians entering retainer or membership arrangements must present the terms clearly, ensure that patients' decisions to join are voluntary, and facilitate transfer of care for any patient who declines to participate; if a feasible transfer is not available, the physician should continue care under the patient's existing insurance terms until other arrangements can be made (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 11.2.5, Retainer Practices).

This means a conversion plan needs a defined process for patients who opt out, not just a marketing push for patients who opt in.

Separately, any patient relationship that ends as part of the transition — for example, patients who decline membership and for whom the practice cannot continue insurance-based care — is governed by the AMA's standard for terminating a patient-physician relationship, which requires advance notice sufficient for the patient to secure another physician and active facilitation of that transfer (AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 1.1.5, Terminating a Patient-Physician Relationship). The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' Committee on Ethics similarly frames ending a therapeutic relationship as a last resort after attempts at resolution, paired with institutional responsibilities to both the patient and the physician during the handoff (ACOG Committee Statement No. 3, December 2022).

For OB/GYN practices specifically, this matters because patients may be mid-pregnancy or mid-treatment at the time of conversion, which narrows the window for a clean transition and makes timing one of the first planning variables, not an afterthought.

Billing transparency is a related requirement, not a separate one. The retainer-practices opinion specifically calls for physicians to distinguish charges for membership-related services and amenities from medical services that may still be billed to insurance, so patients understand what the membership fee covers versus what remains reimbursable care. A transition plan should therefore include a plain-language description of what the membership fee buys, built before enrollment conversations begin.

What changes in daily workflow and staffing

Converting the care model changes nearly every clinical workflow in the practice, not just the appointment calendar. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's workflow guidance defines workflow as the sequence of physical and mental tasks performed within and between people and systems — scheduling, documentation, order entry, communication with patients, and billing all count, and AHRQ notes that any change to practice management processes shifts the workflow around it (AHRQ, Workflow Assessment for Health IT Toolkit). A concierge conversion typically lengthens visit times, reduces daily patient volume, and adds after-hours communication expectations, which means scheduling templates, EHR documentation habits, and front-desk check-in processes all need to be redesigned rather than left in place around a new fee structure.

AHRQ's guidance also stresses that workflow should be assessed before implementing a system change, and continued as an ongoing process after go-live, specifically because research shows that delays in patient care, billing, and communication are common consequences of skipping that analysis. For a practice transition, this translates into concrete tasks: mapping how a patient message currently moves from phone call to triage to physician, then redesigning that path for a smaller panel with different access expectations; identifying who handles after-hours calls and how that coverage is staffed; and confirming the EHR and billing system can separate membership charges from any insurance-billed services cleanly, consistent with the transparency obligation described above.

Staffing changes typically follow from the workflow redesign rather than preceding it. A smaller daily patient volume with longer visits changes the ratio of clinical to administrative staff needed, and after-hours coverage models (whether handled internally, through an answering service, or through a partner's triage team) need to be decided before patient communications go out, since enrollment materials will describe what access patients can expect. Office management, staff training on explaining the new model, and EMR reconfiguration are typically the longest lead-time items in a conversion timeline, which is why sequencing them early — before patient notification — tends to produce a smoother launch than announcing the change and building the infrastructure afterward.

Questions this guide answers

Can a physician simply switch existing patients to a membership fee without notice?

No. AMA ethics guidance on retainer practices requires that patients be told the terms clearly, that joining be voluntary, and that physicians facilitate a transfer of care for anyone who declines; where no transfer is feasible, care should continue under the patient's existing insurance until other arrangements are made. This means a conversion needs a documented opt-out pathway before any notice goes to patients.

How much notice do patients need before a practice model change ends an insurance-based relationship?

AMA guidance on terminating a patient-physician relationship requires notice given long enough in advance for the patient to secure another physician, along with active facilitation of that transfer. The exact timeframe isn't fixed by the opinion itself, so practices typically set a notice period based on local specialist availability and any patients' active treatment status, particularly relevant in OB/GYN care involving pregnancy.

Does workflow redesign need to happen before or after patients are notified of a conversion?

Workflow assessment should happen before the change is implemented, according to AHRQ's health IT workflow guidance, because retrofitting scheduling, documentation, and communication processes after launch tends to produce delays in care, billing, and communication. Mapping the new patient-to-physician communication path and staffing model before notifying patients allows enrollment materials to describe access accurately.
