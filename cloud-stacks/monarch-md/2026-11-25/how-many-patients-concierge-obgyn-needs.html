How Many Patients Does a Concierge OB/GYN Need? Panel Size Considerations

What factors actually determine a workable concierge OB/GYN panel size, and what to check before estimating a target number for your own practice.

There is no single panel-size number that works for every concierge OB/GYN practice, because the right figure depends on membership pricing, visit length, overhead, and how much of the existing patient base is likely to convert. Physicians evaluating this model often start with a number they have heard quoted informally, then discover it does not match their own economics once they run the math on their specific fee structure and fixed costs. The more useful exercise is understanding which variables drive panel size, so a physician can build an estimate grounded in their own practice rather than borrowing someone else's figure.

This guide walks through the inputs that matter most and the checks worth doing before committing to a target.

What actually determines a workable panel size

Panel size in a membership practice is primarily a function of three numbers: the annual membership fee, the fixed and variable costs of running the practice, and the amount of physician time each member is expected to consume. A higher membership fee generally allows a smaller panel to cover costs, while a lower fee requires more members to reach the same revenue target. Test how different appointment lengths would affect the time available for charting, messages, follow-up and urgent scheduling. Include work that happens outside the face-to-face visit. Ask the scheduling team which commitments it can meet under an ordinary week and which would become difficult when staff are away.

Use the practice’s actual appointment ledger to distinguish time already committed from blocks the team can adjust. Identify the consequences of shifting an already booked visit. Physicians should also factor in how many clinical days per week they plan to work, since a three or four day clinical week changes total annual encounter capacity compared with a traditional five day schedule. Finally, attrition and conversion assumptions matter: not every existing patient will join a membership model, and new patient acquisition timelines affect how quickly a practice reaches a sustainable panel after launch.

What to check before estimating your own number

A realistic panel estimate should start with the physician's own patient base rather than an industry average, because conversion willingness varies by geography, patient demographics, and how clearly the change is communicated. A survey can help the practice find questions about membership that need an answer. Record who was contacted, what the survey actually asked and when it ran. Ask the practice’s advisers how to account for patients who did not respond and for commitments that will continue under any model. This sequencing matters: a panel target set before patient interest is measured is a guess, while one set afterward is an estimate grounded in evidence.

Physicians should also separate the question of minimum viable panel size, the number needed to cover costs, from target panel size, the number that reflects a sustainable, well-paced practice once growth stabilizes. These are rarely the same figure, and conflating them can lead to premature conclusions about whether the model is financially workable. Workflow capacity is another practical check often overlooked in early planning. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality notes that any change to practice operations, including the shift to a new care model, changes the workflow tied to clinical and administrative tasks, and that workflow should be assessed before implementation rather than discovered afterward (digital.ahrq.gov).

Applying this to panel planning means testing whether scheduling, documentation, and after-hours coverage can actually support the target number of members at the intended visit length, not just whether the math works on a spreadsheet. A panel size that looks sustainable in a financial model can still be unworkable if the day-to-day workflow was not designed around it. Physicians weighing this decision should also consider how patient continuity obligations interact with panel transitions, since any patients who do not join the membership model still require a documented transfer of care under professional ethics guidance before the relationship can end (code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org).

Building that transition cost and timeline into early panel planning avoids surprises once the conversion process is underway. Treating panel size as a derived output of membership fee, visit capacity, workflow, and documented patient interest, rather than as a starting assumption, produces a far more durable plan.

Questions this guide answers

Is there a standard panel size for a concierge OB/GYN practice?

No. Panel size depends on membership fee, visit length, overhead, and conversion rates, so figures vary by practice rather than following one universal standard.

How should attrition affect panel size planning?

Expect some existing patients not to convert, and factor documented transfer-of-care timelines into launch planning rather than assuming full conversion.
