mobile IV wellness decision support British Columbia: Preparation Checklist 3

Prepare records, setting details, product questions, consent prompts, and an escalation plan for an individual mobile IV assessment in British Columbia.

Preparation should improve the quality of an assessment, not create an expectation that administration will occur. This checklist organizes information a practitioner may need and questions a reader can ask without self-diagnosing or choosing ingredients. It covers medicines, allergies, official product records, professional identity, privacy, infection prevention, consent, documentation, adverse-event readiness, and escalation. It does not recommend a treatment, dose, combination, or level of care. For severe or rapidly worsening symptoms, contact 9-1-1 or emergency services rather than waiting for a mobile provider. Content assessed September 1, 2026. The publisher must reconfirm the linked regulatory and clinical material before publication and with every monthly content cycle.

Complete before confirming the visit

□ Write a current medicine list. Include prescriptions, non-prescription medicines, vitamins, supplements, and natural health products, with labels or records available where practical. Do not omit an item because it seems unrelated. □ Record allergies and earlier reactions. Note what happened, when, and what product or substance was suspected. Include previous difficulties with injections, infusions, dressings, adhesives, or antiseptics for the practitioner to consider. □ Summarize relevant health information and recent changes. Let the assessing professional determine significance rather than using an online checklist to rule yourself in. Pregnancy or breastfeeding considerations, current symptoms, recent care, and known conditions may affect questions or the appropriate setting. □ Obtain provider details. Request the attending person’s name and professional designation, the regulator under which they practise, and a way to verify current registration. Ask who assesses, who is responsible for an order if one is required, who administers, and who handles follow-up. □ Request exact proposed product details. Gather product names, active ingredients, DINs or NPNs where applicable, and the stated purpose. Use Health Canada’s Drug Product Database or Licensed Natural Health Products Database to confirm the matching record. Treat lookup results as identification information, not proof of personal suitability, benefit, intravenous indication, or safe combination. □ Review fees and cancellation terms. Establish whether assessment, travel, supplies, observation, records, and follow-up are included. Ask what happens financially when assessment leads to postponement or no administration.

Check at the location before consent

□ Protect privacy. Identify who is present, who might enter, and whether health history and consent can be discussed without an employer, host, hotel employee, colleague, or event guest listening. Ask for private communication when needed. □ Inspect the practical setup through questions. Ask the practitioner what they need for lighting, a clean surface, hand hygiene, equipment security, sharps disposal, waste removal, and freedom from interruptions. The practitioner—not the customer—must determine whether conditions support the proposed activity. □ Confirm identity and roles again. Scheduling changes can alter who attends. Verify the practitioner and clarify any remote prescriber, supervising professional, dispatcher, or other participant. Ask how communication among them is recorded. □ Reconcile products against the plan. Check that the names and identifiers discussed match the products presented. Ask how storage, integrity, expiry, preparation, and documentation are verified. Do not proceed merely because packaging looks clinical or an ingredient is familiar. □ Have the consent conversation. Request a plain-language explanation of purpose, process, material risks, alternatives, uncertainties, and the option not to receive the service. Ask questions about interactions and contraindications based on the complete medicine and allergy review. Consent should be voluntary and can be reconsidered; prior payment, group participation, or completed setup does not answer for you. □ Confirm monitoring and stop criteria. Ask what will be observed, how concerns are recognized, which findings stop the process, and when another care setting is required.

Retain after the decision or administration

□ Save the service record. Request appropriate documentation of the date, location, practitioner, assessment outcome, products and identifiers, amounts administered if any, relevant observations, reactions, advice, and follow-up contact. If nothing was administered, keep the explanation and any referral information that was provided. □ Know the adverse-event route. Ask whom to contact for a non-urgent concern, when that contact is available, and what details to provide. Establish how the practitioner would summon emergency help and transfer product and assessment information. Do not use a commercial callback when symptoms appear emergent; call 9-1-1 or emergency services. □ Avoid converting the experience into a self-directed future protocol. A prior uneventful visit does not establish ongoing suitability, and a previous reaction deserves disclosure at every later assessment. Update medicines, supplements, allergies, conditions, symptoms, and product records each time. □ Evaluate claims separately from records. Feeling different afterward does not prove a marketed explanation. Promises involving hangover recovery, athletic performance, immune support, energy, detoxification, weight loss, anti-aging, NAD+, prevention, cure, or guaranteed hydration should not be entered into the record as established outcomes without appropriate evidence. □ Preserve the right to pause. Missing provider information, unclear products, incomplete consent, unsuitable surroundings, interaction questions, or weak escalation arrangements are reasons to request clarification. This preparation document supports conversation; it is not medical advice and cannot decide whether care should proceed.

Questions this guide answers

Should preparation assume that an infusion will go ahead?

No. Preparation supports assessment and informed consent; the result may be clarification, postponement, referral, refusal, or no administration.

What records are useful after a visit?

Ask for appropriate documentation of the practitioner, date, products and identifiers, amounts if administered, observations, reactions, instructions, and follow-up route.
