mobile IV wellness decision support British Columbia: Preparation Checklist

Prepare accurate health information, verify provider and product records, assess the mobile space, and define consent and emergency plans before a visit.

Preparation should improve the quality of a clinical decision, not help someone qualify for a predetermined package. Gather accurate information, request product details, verify the professionals involved, and make the mobile location workable. Do not change prescriptions, supplements, food, or fluid intake merely to follow a generic wellness tip. Individual instructions must come from an appropriate health professional who knows the circumstances. This decision framework is educational and is not medical advice or a recommendation concerning an ingredient, quantity, treatment, or appointment. Reviewed September 1, 2026; all health and regulatory content needs fresh validation before publication and at least whenever reused monthly.

Prepare information before the clinician arrives

Create a current list of prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, vitamins, minerals, herbal or natural health products, and recently stopped substances. Include names, strengths when known, schedules, and last doses. Record medication, food, latex, adhesive, and other relevant allergies, describing what reaction occurred rather than writing only “allergic.” Note previous IV problems, fainting, adverse reactions, difficult access, and recent procedures. Do not omit information because it seems unrelated to a wellness package; the assessing professional decides relevance. Prepare a concise health and symptom history: what prompted consideration, when it began, whether it is improving or worsening, recent fluid and food intake, vomiting or diarrhea, fever, urine changes, and any advice already received. Include pertinent diagnoses, pregnancy or breastfeeding considerations, and contact details for usual care when appropriate. This is not a self-assessment tool. If symptoms are severe, sudden, or alarming, contact 9-1-1 or emergency services. Never wait for a commercial provider to arrive during an emergency. Ask for the proposed products in writing. For each, obtain the proper name, brand if relevant, strength, form, amount, route, manufacturer, and asserted DIN or NPN. Use the matching Health Canada database to inspect the exact record. Check spelling, status, dosage form, route, and strength rather than merely confirming that a number returns a result. A database match does not demonstrate that a combination, dose, intravenous route, marketed benefit, or use for a particular person is endorsed. Identify everyone with a clinical role. Request their complete names and professional designations, then verify current registration through the relevant regulator. Ask who assesses, who authorizes the plan, who administers it, and who provides follow-up. Nursing practice may involve medication standards, orders, or autonomous scope depending on the circumstances. The responsible professional should explain the applicable basis without expecting a reader to infer it from advertising.

Prepare the setting, consent discussion, and exit plan

Choose a space with adequate light, stable seating, a clean usable surface, reasonable temperature, handwashing access, and privacy from household members, coworkers, guests, or hotel personnel. Plan for pets and children to remain elsewhere. Confirm parking, building entry, elevator access, unit information, and mobile reception. Ask whether the provider has additional environmental requirements. Do not attempt to create a clinical area with unapproved household cleaning methods; follow the provider’s stated process and ask how their infection-prevention procedures work. Protect confidentiality. Decide who, if anyone, may hear the assessment or remain present. For workplace and event visits, ask how appointments are separated, how records and invoices are kept from organizers, and where supplies are stored. In a hotel, clarify access and waste removal without unnecessarily disclosing health information. The recipient should be able to communicate privately with the professional even when another person paid. Write consent questions in advance: What is being proposed and why? What are material risks and uncertainties? What alternatives include declining? What observations will be taken? What could lead to postponement, modification, stopping, or referral? How can consent be withdrawn? Ask how unexpected findings affect price and care. Consent must remain voluntary, including when a deposit has been paid or peers are participating. Prepare for the end of the visit. Request a record of products, amounts, time, relevant identifiers, administering professional, observations, and aftercare directions. Confirm a reachable clinical contact and distinguish routine questions from urgent or emergency action. Ask how adverse events are documented and reviewed. Ensure responders could enter the building if required, but understand that emergency planning cannot guarantee safety. Use a final stop-or-proceed check immediately before administration: information complete; professional identity confirmed; products reconciled; questions answered; privacy adequate; monitoring explained; emergency route clear; consent still present. Any unresolved discrepancy is a reason to pause. Preparation is successful even if the resulting clinical decision is to defer, redirect, or decline the appointment.

Questions this guide answers

Should someone stop a prescription before a wellness appointment?

Not on generic advice. Disclose all medicines and obtain individualized direction from an appropriate health professional.

What product facts should be requested in advance?

Request proper and brand names, strength, dosage form, proposed amount, route, manufacturer, and any claimed DIN or NPN.

Can preparation appropriately end with cancellation?

Yes. Deferring or declining is reasonable when assessment, identity, products, consent, setting, monitoring, or escalation questions remain unresolved.
