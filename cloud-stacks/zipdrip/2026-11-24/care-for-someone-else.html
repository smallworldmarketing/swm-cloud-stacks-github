Questions When You Are Booking a Visit for Someone Else

Clarify who receives appointment details, who speaks privately with the clinician, and how decisions and changes will be communicated.

You may be asked to coordinate a mobile IV wellness visit for a relative, guest or housemate. Start by deciding how to help the person who would receive the service get the information they need. The caller, payer, and care recipient may want different things. Separating their roles can make the appointment easier to discuss and leave space for the recipient's own questions. This is general information, not medical advice. Reviewed on 23 November 2026.

Ask the recipient what help they want

Before contacting a service, ask whether the person wants help with scheduling, a list of questions, accessibility arrangements, or a way to speak directly with the provider. Have them decide which information you can pass along. They may want you to share a callback number but choose to discuss their medical history themselves. Write down the requested help in the recipient's own terms. If you are making arrangements for someone who does not want a visit, treat that as a reason to stop the booking conversation and seek appropriate guidance if needed.

Separate the messages

Ask the booking team to identify which messages go to the person who requested the appointment and which go to the prospective recipient. Confirm who receives a cancellation notice when the date changes and how the practitioner reaches the person whose health information will be reviewed. If different people use the same email address or phone, ask what other contact methods the service can offer. For a private conversation about medicines or a past reaction, a previously arranged callback might be easier for the recipient to control than a message thread with several participants.

Arrange a private chance to ask questions

The person receiving the service may value your help but want to ask about proposed ingredients, professional roles, or a past concern without an audience. Ask how to reserve that time and where the conversation could happen. Find out whether the person can ask to change who is in the room. The BCCNM consent standard describes a person's right to make decisions about their care and to decline or withdraw consent. A support person can help track questions and repeat back logistical details with permission. The practitioner can clarify consent arrangements when a representative is involved.

Ask who checks what

Ask which professional gathers the health history, reviews medications and allergies, explains the proposal, and would be responsible during the visit. If the recipient has a medication list, ask the practitioner which format would be useful and how to send it through the intended channel. A booking caller can prepare the list with the person's permission. Leave interpretation and any decision about dose, products, interactions or suitability with the qualified professional. Ask what happens if the information is incomplete or if the recipient would prefer to contact their usual care team before deciding.

Agree how changes will be communicated

Choose an example such as a different arrival time or a different proposed product. Ask who would contact the recipient, what details would be explained, and how the recipient could ask further questions. If the payer needs to be informed about a refund or a changed charge, agree which details are purely administrative. A single contact can relay an arrival window while the recipient discusses the clinical change privately. Write down how the provider will record important changes and how the person could request a copy for their own files.

Keep the support role useful after the visit

With permission, save the names of the booking contact and the clinician, a receipt if appropriate, and an agreed way to handle later questions. Ask the practitioner to explain the follow-up and escalation plan directly to the recipient, including what to do if there are urgent concerns. In an emergency, call 9-1-1 or local emergency services. If the person decides to pause, your remaining job might simply be to cancel the booking, record who received the cancellation, and return any documents they shared so they can use them in another conversation.
