Preparing for a Pittsburgh Employment Attorney

Preparing for a Pittsburgh Employment Attorney. Practical preparation and next-step questions for Pittsburgh, PA.

A useful provider conversation is not limited to asking, “What will this cost?” Scope determines what a quoted fee covers, which decisions remain yours, and whether the provider is discussing advice, negotiation, an agency matter, litigation, or only document review. Prepare the same core questions for every consultation so comparisons are fair. Do not assume contingency representation, a free meeting, a particular response time, or acceptance of a matter. This checklist supports discussions with qualified providers without recommending one or interpreting an agreement. Informational only, not legal advice.

Questions to ask before booking a Pittsburgh attorney

When contacting an employment attorney in Pittsburgh, explain the type of employee-side help you are seeking and what the first meeting needs to accomplish. A severance review, an agency-process discussion, and possible litigation representation are different scopes. Ask what is included in the consultation and which questions would require a separate engagement. Confirm who will conduct the meeting, what information is needed for a conflict check, and how confidential documents should be submitted. Do not send an entire personnel or medical file through an unverified channel merely to obtain a booking.

A concise description of the employer, the issue, relevant locations, and any approaching date can help the office explain the next intake step. Ask about fees, expenses, cancellation terms, and whether a written scope is supplied. Do not infer an engagement's terms from a general slogan about payment. Keep your own calendar of unresolved dates until responsibility is expressly established. The aim is an informed booking decision, not a prediction that a particular provider will take the case or obtain a result.

Define the work you are asking about

Write the immediate objective in one sentence before discussing price. It might be understanding a severance proposal, evaluating possible agency routes, preparing for an internal report, reviewing a non-compete, organizing a wage concern, or obtaining advice after termination. Ask the provider to describe the proposed task in equally concrete language. “Review” could mean reading one document and holding a call, or it could include revisions, negotiations, research, and follow-up. Those are materially different engagements. Use a scope checklist: which people or entities would be represented; which issues are included; which jurisdictions are being considered; what documents will be read; whether written analysis is provided; whether the provider will communicate with an employer or agency; and what event ends the engagement.

If Pennsylvania and New Jersey both appear in the facts, ask whether the scope includes evaluating both states or only identifying the need for additional review. Federal coverage also should not be presumed merely because a federal agency publishes relevant general guidance. Clarify exclusions. A provider discussing employment discrimination may not be handling benefits, workers’ compensation, unemployment, union proceedings, taxes, immigration consequences, personal injury, or an appeal. Ask how overlapping issues will be identified and whether separate counsel may be needed. Never infer coverage from a firm name or a broad practice description.

Request a written engagement agreement and read it before relying on the relationship. A consultation, document intake, or conflict check is not necessarily an agreement to represent you.

Ask how fees and expenses operate

Request the fee structure for each proposed phase. Possibilities may include hourly work, a fixed fee for a defined task, a contingent fee, a hybrid arrangement, or another structure, but no structure should be assumed or treated as universally available. Ask what triggers billing, how time is recorded, whether minimum increments apply, when invoices are issued, what deposit or replenishment terms apply, and what happens if the task changes. If a contingent arrangement is offered, ask which recoveries are included, how the percentage is determined, and how termination of the relationship is addressed in the written terms.

Separate professional fees from expenses. Filing charges, transcripts, service, records, travel, expert work, mediation, electronic discovery, and other items may be handled differently. Ask who approves an expense, who advances it, when reimbursement is due, and what happens to expenses regardless of outcome. Do not rely on generalized statements about what employment cases “usually” cost. The relevant answer is the written arrangement for the defined work. Discuss decision points that could expand cost: an employer response, a new document set, an agency request, emergency motion practice, discovery, settlement work, or trial preparation.

Ask whether you will receive a revised estimate or scope proposal before added work begins. Estimates are planning tools, not outcome guarantees. Also ask what client work can reduce avoidable time, such as providing an indexed chronology, complete agreements, selected communications, and a question list instead of an unsorted device export.

Confirm communication and responsibility boundaries

Ask who will be the primary contact, who may perform work, how urgent material should be delivered, and which communication channel is approved. Learn what updates are routine and what requires a scheduled discussion. Do not send confidential information until the provider gives secure intake instructions and explains whether an attorney-client relationship exists. Avoid copying a current employer or using an employer-controlled account or device for sensitive communications. Identify responsibilities on both sides. The provider may require complete and accurate information, preservation of records, prompt responses, attendance, and notice of new events.

The client may expect an explanation of options, material developments, requested approvals, and billing. Ask who tracks agency or court dates after an engagement begins. Until responsibility is expressly accepted, continue protecting your own interests and checking official instructions. For discrimination process context, the EEOC explains that a charge is a signed statement asserting employment discrimination and requesting remedial action. That is distinct from merely asking a question or completing preliminary intake. The precise route, available forum, and timing require current facts. Filing periods can change with the law asserted, place, employer, and events, so never use a consultation-cost discussion as a reason to postpone checking current agency guidance.

End by comparing providers on defined scope, understandable terms, relevant experience questions, communication fit, conflicts, and your confidence in the working process—not on promised results.

Questions this guide answers

Is a consultation automatically an agreement to provide representation?

No. Intake, a conflict check, or a consultation may occur before a provider accepts a defined engagement. Confirm representation and scope in writing.

What should a cost comparison include besides fees?

Compare included tasks, exclusions, expenses, staffing, communication, phase changes, client responsibilities, and the event that ends the engagement.
