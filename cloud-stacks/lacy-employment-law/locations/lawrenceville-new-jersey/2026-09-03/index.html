About The Lacy Employment Law Firm

Workplace concerns rarely arrive in a neat legal category. A termination may follow a request for leave; a pay dispute may involve disputed work time or classification; unwelcome conduct may be followed by a schedule change. Begin by separating what happened, when it happened, who participated, and what records exist. Then identify whether the work occurred in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, or several locations. That sequence helps a reader choose reliable information without prematurely deciding that conduct was unlawful. This guide explains preparation and official processes; it does not assess any person’s rights or promise an outcome.

Sort the concern before choosing a route

Create a short working description based on observable events. Useful categories include hiring or promotion decisions, harassment, retaliation after a report, disability accommodation, family or medical leave, unpaid wages, overtime, worker classification, severance, non-compete restrictions, and discharge. Categories may overlap, so do not force every event into one label. Preserve the original words used in emails, policies, evaluations, pay records, or meeting notes. Distinguish a document you received at the time from a summary created later. Location and employer facts can affect which rules and forums are relevant. Record the employing entity shown on pay statements, the usual worksite, any remote-work locations, approximate workforce information if known, and whether a union agreement or public-employment system may apply.

Federal material should be treated as relevant only where the cited federal law covers the worker and employer. Pennsylvania and New Jersey materials should remain tied to the named state. The Pennsylvania Human Relations Commission publishes employment-discrimination information, while the New Jersey Division on Civil Rights describes its civil-rights role. Their pages are starting points for current terminology and instructions, not substitutes for individualized analysis. Use a neutral timeline rather than a conclusion-led narrative. Include favorable, unfavorable, and ordinary events; selective records can obscure sequence and credibility. Keep lawful copies of materials already available to you, but do not access restricted systems, take proprietary information, or record conversations without first understanding applicable rules.

Match the question to an official information source

For discrimination-charge process questions, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission explains how filing works and separately publishes time-limit information. The agency warns that filing periods depend on circumstances and that internal grievance, arbitration, mediation, or union processes generally do not extend the charge-filing clock. Readers should check the current agency pages promptly rather than calculate a personal deadline from a general article. Deadlines can vary by law, forum, employer, location, and facts. For retaliation literacy, compare the original activity, the employer’s knowledge, later actions, timing, and documented explanations without assuming that sequence alone proves a violation.

The EEOC describes participation and opposition concepts under the laws it enforces. For pay questions, the U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division provides Fair Labor Standards Act information concerning minimum wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and youth employment where federal coverage applies. Its employment-relationship fact sheet also discusses factors used when employee status is disputed. Titles such as “contractor” or “manager” should be recorded, but a label alone should not be treated as the answer. For leave, the Department of Labor’s FMLA employee guide outlines federal eligibility, qualifying reasons, notice, certification, and restoration concepts.

Coverage and eligibility require facts; not every illness, employer, or worker is covered. Save leave requests, responses, certification communications, attendance records, and benefit notices. When disability accommodation is also involved, keep the two processes distinct in the timeline even if the same health condition appears in both.

Choose a proportionate next step

The next step may be gathering missing records, reading an agency’s current instructions, asking a focused workplace question, using an internal process, contacting an agency, or seeking legal advice. Compare routes by purpose. An internal report may notify an employer and create a record, but it is not necessarily the same as filing with a government agency. An agency intake may provide a public enforcement path, but it is not automatically a private consultation. A lawyer can discuss facts, agreements, risks, and possible routes, yet contacting one does not guarantee representation, fees, timing, or results.

Before acting, identify the immediate decision. Examples are whether to sign a severance document, how to preserve a fast-changing record, whether an agency instruction applies, or what to ask before an interview. Do not edit an agreement or rely on a generalized interpretation of a non-compete. Note the document’s stated response date, preserve the complete version, and seek qualified advice where appropriate. Questions this publication answers

Does an internal complaint replace an agency filing?

Not necessarily. Internal and agency processes have different purposes, and an internal process generally should not be assumed to extend an external filing period. Check current agency instructions promptly.

Should one workplace event be assigned to only one category?

No. Leave, accommodation, retaliation, pay, harassment, and termination facts can overlap. Preserve the sequence first and avoid deciding the legal category prematurely.
