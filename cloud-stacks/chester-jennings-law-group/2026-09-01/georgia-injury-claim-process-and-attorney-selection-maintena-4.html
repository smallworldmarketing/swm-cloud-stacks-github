Georgia injury claim process and attorney selection: Maintenance Decisions 3

Questions for maintaining a Georgia injury file through changing records, communications, court stages, and eventual permanent closeout.

A well-maintained file shows what arrived, where it came from, what remains unresolved, and who owns the next action. It does not need to argue the case. Maintenance becomes especially important as medical, employment, insurance, legal, and court materials accumulate on different schedules. The reader’s decision is what to update routinely, what to escalate promptly, and what to retain permanently. This page gives organizational questions, not legal advice about evidence, disclosure, liens, settlement, or destruction. Ask counsel before discarding potentially relevant material or changing preservation practices, and follow responsible professionals’ instructions for health, financial, insurance, and official records.

What should the active file reveal at a glance?

Maintain a master index with item number, date, source, short description, original location, working-copy location, confidentiality level, and follow-up status. Add separate registers for communications, expenses, medical providers, employment records, insurance correspondence, public-record requests, and legal documents. The front page should identify the most recent update, next scheduled event, unanswered questions, and the person responsible for each action. Avoid a single miscellaneous folder; it hides missing attachments and duplicate versions. Use stable filenames that include date, source, and document type while preserving untouched originals. Version notes should explain redactions, annotations, conversions, or combined PDFs. Never overwrite the only copy. Back up the working file through a reasonably secure method suited to its sensitivity, and control access rather than sharing a universal link. Review contact details after moves, phone changes, employment changes, or provider changes. Notify the appropriate person through the agreed channel without broadcasting sensitive information. Reconcile the communication log against email, portal messages, and letters so that a telephone note is not mistaken for an official document. If an entry expresses memory or interpretation, label it accordingly. Maintenance should preserve provenance: who created the record, when it was received, and whether it is complete. When a gap appears, record the gap and request route; do not manufacture a replacement or assume the missing item’s contents.

Which changes require more than a routine update?

Escalate any new document with a printed response date, court caption, service information, release, authorization, settlement language, recorded-statement request, lien assertion, government sender, or demand for sensitive data. Escalation means obtaining review from the qualified person; it does not imply a prescribed answer. New symptoms or care questions go to an appropriate clinician. Policy administration belongs with the insurer or responsible regulator, while interpretation of legal consequences belongs with counsel. A new potential party, changed incident account, returned records request, or notice that material may no longer be retained can also warrant prompt legal discussion. Preserve the source communication and the envelope or transmission metadata. If litigation begins, add a pleadings and orders register organized by the court’s caption and filing information. As of September 1, 2026, Georgia Courts publishes eAccess guidance for court records. Use that official access information for orientation, not to interpret docket events or calculate an obligation. Counsel should explain filing, service, discovery, deposition, expert, mediation, trial, and appeal stages as they apply. Not every case includes every stage. Keep discovery materials distinct from public court records because access, confidentiality, and use may differ. Do not post documents online merely because one item is publicly accessible. Digital preservation also does not authorize surveillance, unauthorized access, or collection from restricted property.

How should closeout records be designed for later questions?

Closeout should produce a durable, understandable package rather than an unexplained archive. Ask counsel which original materials will be returned, what copies the office will retain, in what format, for how long under applicable policy, and how later requests are made. Obtain and review the applicable final accounting or settlement statement, if one exists, with entries for funds, fees, expenses, asserted liens or balances, distributions, and unresolved items. This guide does not determine whether an entry is correct or who must pay it. Ask the responsible lawyer, provider, administrator, tax professional, or financial professional about issues within that person’s role. Preserve the signed representation agreement, amendments, key correspondence, final court papers, releases or other executed documents, closing accounting, proof of distributions, lien-resolution material, record-return inventory, and contact details for future requests. Record whether originals were returned and who received them. Do not adopt an automatic deletion date for the personal archive based on a generic article. Legal, tax, medical, estate, insurance, and record-retention considerations vary. Ask qualified advisers what should remain permanently and what may eventually be disposed of securely. If representation ends before the broader matter ends, create a transition sheet listing pending dates, unresolved tasks, file-transfer status, former and new contacts, and outstanding financial questions. A clean closeout preserves future explainability; it cannot certify that every legal or financial issue has been resolved.

Questions this guide answers

Should duplicate working files replace digital originals?

No. Preserve originals with their context and metadata, then identify altered, combined, annotated, or redacted working versions.

Does public court access make every related document suitable for reposting?

No. Privacy, confidentiality, context, and differing access rules still matter.

Can a generic guide set a destruction date for an injury file?

No. Retention considerations vary; ask the appropriate legal, tax, medical, insurance, or records professional before disposal.
