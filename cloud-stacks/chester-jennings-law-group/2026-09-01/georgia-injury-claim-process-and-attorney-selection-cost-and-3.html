Georgia injury claim process and attorney selection: Cost and Scope Questions 2

Use a structured review of fee terms, expenses, liens, authority, staffing, and file-transfer provisions before choosing Georgia counsel.

The practical cost of representation cannot be understood from a percentage alone. A proposed agreement may address attorney compensation, case expenses, third-party claims, decision authority, staffing, withdrawal, and the handling of records when representation ends. Those subjects should be read together because each can affect the client’s responsibilities. This framework does not supply a typical fee, interpret a contract, or predict a net recovery. Terms differ, and their legal effect depends on the actual agreement and circumstances. Before signing, ask the proposing lawyer to explain unclear language and consider independent legal advice when needed. Keep a complete copy of every version reviewed.

Review the agreement through four connected lenses

First examine compensation language. Locate every provision describing how the lawyer would be paid and whether the percentage changes at a defined stage. Ask what event activates each provision and what amount serves as the calculation base. Do not assume that “contingency” answers questions about expenses, liens, taxes, or what happens if representation ends. Request a worked hypothetical using invented numbers solely to demonstrate the agreement’s arithmetic, not to estimate the actual matter. Second examine costs. Identify which expenditures the agreement contemplates, who may authorize them, whether approval is required above a threshold, how they are tracked, and when the client receives an accounting. Possible categories may include records, filing, service, transcripts, experts, travel, copying, or other litigation-related items, but their presence and allocation cannot be assumed. Ask what happens to incurred costs if no recovery occurs, the lawyer withdraws, or the client changes counsel. The written agreement—not an advertisement or general conversation—should be the focus. Third examine third-party obligations. Medical providers, benefit programs, insurers, or others may assert reimbursement or lien-related interests, but their existence and amount are matter-specific. Ask whether the proposed scope includes identifying, verifying, disputing, or resolving such claims; who communicates with the claimant; and how unresolved amounts appear on a closing statement. The lawyer should explain what work is included and what requires separate advice. No planning guide can determine whether a particular lien is valid. Fourth examine scope and authority. Determine which claim, parties, court stages, or appeals are included. Ask whether related property damage, benefits, probate, employment, criminal, family, bankruptcy, or tax questions fall outside the engagement. Confirm that settlement decisions remain subject to the agreement and applicable professional duties, and ask how offers, major expenses, pleadings, and strategic choices would be communicated.

Use decision gates before accepting the terms

Gate one is comprehension. Can the reader explain, in plain words, the payment formula, expense treatment, covered work, excluded work, and ending provisions? Mark each uncertain clause and request a specific explanation. A reassuring summary is not a substitute for reconciling the summary with the document. Avoid signing with blank spaces or missing attachments. Note the date, retain the complete proposed agreement, and record who answered each question. Gate two is operational fit. Ask who will perform routine work, which lawyer has supervisory responsibility, how urgent messages are handled, and what update rhythm can reasonably be expected. Determine whether the office uses a portal, email, telephone, mail, or another channel, then consider accessibility and privacy. Clarify what the client must provide and how address, employment, health-provider, or insurance changes should be reported. Gate three is exit readiness. Read provisions about discharge, withdrawal, earned fees, costs, file transfer, retaining copies, successor counsel, and unresolved court dates. Asking does not signal distrust; it tests whether both sides understand a difficult transition before it occurs. Request an explanation of what records the client can obtain and in what format. Never assume a file moves automatically or that changing lawyers eliminates earlier contractual questions. Gate four is verification and reflection. Confirm the lawyer’s Georgia licence information through the official member directory and record the access date. If public disciplinary history is relevant to the decision, distinguish the Bar’s disciplinary process from online commentary and ask the Bar about its official information. Verification does not endorse a lawyer or establish suitability. Compare written answers from candidates using the same categories, remove outcome promises from the scoring, and choose only after the scope and working relationship are understandable. Case-specific contract questions belong with a licensed lawyer.

Questions this guide answers

Is the contingency percentage the complete cost picture?

Not necessarily. The agreement may separately address expenses, third-party claims, calculation methods, changing stages, and termination. Review those provisions together.

Should a reader assume an appeal is included?

No. Ask which claims and stages are within scope and whether trial, post-judgment work, or appeal would require another agreement.
