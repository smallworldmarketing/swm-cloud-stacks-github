Georgia injury claim process and attorney selection: Questions Before Booking

A booking checklist covering lawyer verification, intake privacy, file responsibility, relevant scope, fees, and consultation readiness.

Before booking a consultation, a short screening conversation can prevent mismatched expectations and unnecessary disclosure. The objective is not to obtain a case evaluation from reception staff. It is to verify the lawyer, understand the intake process, confirm that the office is prepared to discuss the general subject, and learn what information the meeting requires. No appointment guarantees representation or a particular outcome. Keep initial details limited, use the office’s verified channel, and reserve legal conclusions for a licensed Georgia lawyer who has reviewed the relevant facts.

Verify identity, intake boundaries, and meeting purpose

Ask for the full name of the lawyer expected to participate and verify that person through the State Bar of Georgia member directory. Confirm that the record matches the intended individual rather than relying on a similar name. The directory, reviewed September 1, 2026, supplies public membership information; it does not recommend the lawyer or validate a service claim. Ask what the meeting is designed to cover. Is it an initial information exchange, a consultation with a lawyer, or a later engagement discussion? Who will attend, and who reviews material beforehand? Request an explanation of when the office considers an attorney-client relationship created. An appointment confirmation, automated reply, or uploaded file should not be treated as proof of representation without clarification. Confirm the approved way to send documents. Ask whether the office needs participant names for conflict checking, a short chronology, insurance correspondence, or a document awaiting review. Do not transmit unrelated medical history, full financial records, intimate photographs, account credentials, or original physical items simply because an upload button exists. For highly sensitive or assault-related matters, ask about safe contact preferences, privacy, and how preliminary information is limited. Describe the incident category without deciding fault. For example: motor-vehicle event, rideshare trip, motorcycle incident, premises event, animal bite, catastrophic injury, death-related inquiry, or assault-related injury. Ask whether the office is currently prepared to discuss that category. This is a scope question, not a demand for case acceptance.

Ask who would do what if representation were offered

Who would be responsible for the file? Which tasks might be performed by another lawyer, paralegal, investigator, or outside professional? Who conducts substantive calls and communicates major developments? If litigation occurs, who would handle depositions, mediation, hearings, or trial? A consultation may not answer every future staffing question, but the office should be able to describe its intended model. Ask how the team receives new correspondence and reports urgent documents. What channel is used for routine questions? How are contact changes recorded? Are updates tied to defined events? Seek a practical system instead of a guaranteed response interval. For a dog-bite inquiry, prepare questions about animal identification, location, ownership or control information, medical care, photographs, witness details, and reports to responsible authorities. These are record categories, not findings that an owner or another person is liable. Health concerns require prompt medical attention, and immediate danger belongs with emergency or animal-control authorities. For catastrophic-injury or wrongful-death discussions, ask what authority documents, family-role information, estate records, medical authorizations, and privacy safeguards may be needed. Do not assume which relative can direct a claim or sign an agreement. The proper person and scope of authority depend on Georgia law and individual facts.

Confirm the consultation agenda and decision documents

Ask whether the consultation will address urgent dates, possible government involvement, additional records, insurance communications, and documents presented for signature. Provide exact correspondence rather than paraphrasing consequential language. The lawyer should review the context before advising about a recorded statement, authorization, release, filing, or settlement proposal. This checklist does not prescribe a response. If the office may offer representation, request time to read the complete agreement. Questions should cover included stages, excluded services, contingency provisions, possible percentage changes, expenses, authority to incur costs, repayment responsibility, lien-related work, withdrawal, client termination, and file return. No fee rate or cost outcome is assumed. Bring a concise chronology, participant list, document inventory, pending-paper list, and consultation questions. Keep originals secure unless the office has arranged to receive them. Flag uncertain dates and possible public entities for prompt analysis. Georgia Courts’ self-help resources, reviewed September 1, 2026, can provide general court orientation but cannot determine a reader’s deadline, proper party, venue, or required filing. Before confirming the booking, write down the lawyer’s name, date, meeting method, attendees, approved submission channel, requested materials, and the office’s statement about whether representation exists. Afterward, compare the explanation with any written agreement. The best booking decision is one that creates a clear next conversation without oversharing or mistaking preliminary contact for a legal commitment.

Questions this guide answers

Does a booked consultation mean the lawyer represents the caller?

Not necessarily. Ask when the attorney-client relationship forms and obtain confirmation of any agreed representation.

What should be shared before the appointment?

Use the verified channel and provide only what the office says is needed for conflict checking and focused preliminary review.

Who may act in a wrongful-death or estate-related inquiry?

Authority depends on Georgia law and the particular circumstances; family relationship alone may not answer the question.
