Checking Roles in a Traffic-Control Proposal

Checking Roles in a Traffic-Control Proposal. Practical preparation and next-step questions for Vancouver, BC.

Choosing among traffic-control options is easier when the buyer compares operating models instead of collecting vague assurances. A field-crew service, planning assignment, lane-closure support, or event-access engagement may overlap, but they do not solve identical problems. The right question is not which label sounds most complete. It is which combination of responsibilities fits the work, the governing authority, the affected road users, and the project team’s own capabilities. The questions below are designed for conversations with multiple providers. They do not establish a layout, authorize a closure, or replace the current official material and site assessment.

Questions that reveal what each option actually includes

Ask, “What problem do you understand us to be solving?” A useful answer should distinguish worker exposure, public movement, work-space needs, access continuity, schedule constraints, and authority requirements. If the answer jumps immediately to devices or personnel without confirming the activity and location, the option may be premature. Ask, “Which planning tasks are included, and which remain with us or another specialist?” Clarify responsibility for risk information, concept development, traffic-management documentation, drawing production, internal review, authority submissions, revisions, implementation scheduling, and field briefings. Planning support can range from organizing inputs to producing a defined deliverable.

Capture the exact boundary in writing. Ask, “How do you determine field resources?” The answer should point to the applicable requirements, approved or accepted project documents where required, hazard assessment, work method, site observations, and qualified supervision. Be cautious of a universal crew or equipment formula offered before the facts are known. Do not request device spacing or setup instructions as part of an early commercial comparison. Ask, “How will walking, cycling, transit, driveway, delivery, and emergency movements be addressed?” Not every interface applies to every site, but the provider should have a method for discovering and coordinating those that do.

Event work also needs clarity about arrival surges, departures, vendors, participants, and interfaces between private property and public roads. Ask, “What authority interaction do you support?” Distinguish advice, preparation, submission assistance, revision support, and actual authority decisions. Confirm the road jurisdiction and any other property controller. No service description should be read as permission to occupy space.

Questions that test field governance and evidence

Ask, “Who has operational responsibility at each stage?” Identify the client contact, site supervisor, traffic-control lead, plan author or reviewer when applicable, and escalation contact. Clarify how instructions flow when construction needs conflict with the documented arrangement. A field worker should not be forced to reconcile competing directions without a defined chain. Ask, “What triggers reassessment?” Strong answers address changed work limits, unexpected traffic, visibility loss, weather, queues, access conflicts, damaged or displaced devices, schedule overruns, and authority direction. Request the communication path and the threshold for pausing work. This is more informative than a broad claim that a team is flexible.

Ask, “What records will we receive?” Depending on scope, useful records may include versions, briefing acknowledgements, inspection or monitoring notes, change logs, incident or near-miss information, photographs where appropriate, and closeout observations. Confirm retention, privacy, ownership, and delivery timing. Documentation should support learning and accountability, not become a substitute for active supervision. Ask, “How are competence and credentials verified?” British Columbia has defined training and certification context for traffic control persons, and BCCSA publishes information about its program. Ask for evidence relevant to the people and roles proposed, while respecting privacy.

A company-level statement does not prove the current status or suitability of every individual. Ask, “How will commercial changes be approved?” Define notice, pricing, authorization, and documentation for extra shifts, revised limits, standby, remobilization, or added coordination. Finally ask for references to the exact official material the provider expects to govern the assignment, including edition or version.

Write down the handoff between separate providers

When planning and field delivery are purchased separately, name the person who transfers the controlling documents and confirms that the receiving team has them. Record the version, the handoff date, and any unresolved question. If a proposal combines those services, identify the same responsibilities within the combined team. Integration can reduce interfaces, but it does not remove the need to know who owns a decision. A short responsibility record is more useful than assuming that every participant has seen the latest attachment.

Questions this guide answers

Should providers be compared using a standard questionnaire?

Yes. Consistent questions expose differences in deliverables, responsibility, change control, records, and authority support.

Does a company-level certification statement verify every assigned worker?

No. Ask for current evidence appropriate to each proposed role and avoid turning a company statement into regulator endorsement.
