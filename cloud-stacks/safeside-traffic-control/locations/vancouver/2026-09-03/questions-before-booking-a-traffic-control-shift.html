Questions Before Booking a Traffic-Control Shift

Questions Before Booking a Traffic-Control Shift. Practical preparation and next-step questions for Vancouver, BC.

Booking event traffic control should follow a clear operating concept, not substitute for one. Public, private, and community events can affect arrivals, departures, pedestrian movement, parking, deliveries, transit, emergency access, and nearby roads. A provider needs enough verified information to define its role, while the organizer must preserve decisions belonging to the venue, road authority, emergency services, or other responsible parties. The questions below create a practical booking record. They do not authorize a closure, promise approval, or dictate a site layout. Use the answers to expose dependencies before confirming dates, costs, or public instructions.

Questions about place, demand, and authority

What are the exact event boundaries, entrances, exits, parking areas, loading locations, gathering points, and public-road interfaces? Which sidewalks, cycling facilities, transit stops, driveways, intersections, or curb spaces might be affected? Who owns or controls each space? Obtain authority confirmation rather than inferring jurisdiction from an address or venue agreement. What is the event timetable, and when are the likely arrival and departure peaks? Include setup, vendor access, volunteer check-in, gates opening, program changes, intermissions, closing, cleanup, and equipment removal. What attendance or vehicle assumptions support planning, and who owns those forecasts?

Avoid converting an estimate into a guarantee. Identify how ticketing, weather, concurrent events, or transit disruption could alter demand. Which movements must remain available? Ask about emergency routes, accessible entrances, resident and business access, transit operations, deliveries, performer or contractor access, and pedestrian connections. Record commitments already communicated to stakeholders and whether they have authority acceptance. If public-road use or closure is contemplated, ask what current submission, permit, notification, professional input, and review process applies. Hiring a traffic-control company does not transfer these decisions automatically.

Questions about service scope and operating roles

What support is actually being booked: planning assistance, event access coordination, public-road traffic control, lane-closure implementation, equipment logistics, site review, documentation, or a defined combination? For each component, ask what deliverable marks completion and what is excluded. Clarify whether plan preparation, authority submission, revisions, and implementation are separate items. No provider should be presumed able to guarantee an external approval. Who directs venue staff, security, parking attendants, volunteers, traffic-control personnel, vendors, and contractors? Where do roles overlap, and who resolves a conflict? Identify the event lead, traffic-control supervisor, venue contact, authority contact where applicable, and emergency liaison.

Document communication channels, briefing times, check-in points, and the person authorized to approve commercial changes. Confirm how trained personnel and any required current credentials will be matched to assigned duties. What controlled documents will field teams receive? Ask for titles, version identifiers, status, and distribution timing. Determine how construction or event changes reach every affected group and how superseded instructions are withdrawn. WorkSafeBC publishes requirements and training information concerning traffic control, while BCCSA describes its Traffic Control Person program. These sources help organizers ask informed questions, but neither certifies the event’s proposed arrangement through a general webpage.

Questions about contingencies, records, and the booking decision

What foreseeable changes have a response process? Consider poor weather, delayed programming, unexpected attendance patterns, blocked access, transit disruption, medical incidents, vehicle breakdowns, or a vendor arriving outside its window. Ask who observes conditions, who receives reports, and which decisions require the authority or a qualified planner. Do not request a generic promise to “handle anything.” Seek named escalation routes and explicit hold points. What records are required before, during, and after the event? Possibilities include authority correspondence, accepted plans, briefings, contact lists, inspection observations, shift logs, approved changes, incident records, and closeout notes.

The exact set depends on applicable duties and project requirements. Establish custody, retention, and privacy expectations before information is collected. Ask when a post-event review will occur and who turns lessons into controlled revisions for a future edition. Before booking, summarize the answers in five headings: confirmed facts, assumptions, provider deliverables, organizer obligations, and external dependencies. Mark unresolved authority or access decisions as hold points rather than burying them in fine print. Compare proposals only after bidders have received the same brief. The appropriate choice should show a credible understanding of the defined scope, interfaces, document controls, competent staffing process, and changing-condition governance.

A signed booking can then reserve agreed support while clearly remaining subject to the permissions and accepted plans the event requires.

Questions this guide answers

Does booking event traffic control authorize use of a public road?

No. Public-road occupation, alteration, or closure remains subject to the applicable authority’s current process and decision.

What should an event organizer define before requesting proposals?

Define event limits, authority, timetable, demand assumptions, affected users, access commitments, requested services, role boundaries, documents, and contingency governance.

Should venue security and traffic-control roles be combined?

They should not be assumed interchangeable. Define each function, reporting line, competence requirement, overlap, and escalation path for the specific event.
