Matching a Vancouver Project Description to a Service Type

A worked scenario showing how to read a Vancouver project's scope and match it to flagging, construction, event, or lane-closure traffic control.

Many people requesting traffic control in Vancouver are not sure which service label fits their project until they write out the actual scope. This article works through a sample project description, line by line, to show how specific details point toward construction traffic control, event traffic control, flagging services, or a lane-closure arrangement. The goal is not to pick a brand name but to recognize which combination of elements belongs in a request before contacting any provider.

The scenario below is deliberately mixed, because many real projects touch more than one category. Reading it carefully is the reader's actual decision: does this job need flaggers only, a documented traffic management plan, or both, and does it change if the dates shift from a single afternoon to several weeks.

The scenario: a block-party-style event next to an active utility dig

Picture a neighbourhood association planning a Saturday street event on a block where a utility contractor is finishing a multi-day trench repair that started earlier in the week. The event needs pedestrian movement control, parking guidance for a loading zone, and sidewalk access for vendors. The utility work, running concurrently a block away, needs lane protection for equipment staging and worker safety during daylight hours.

This is really two separate scopes wearing one calendar date. The event piece is about managing arrivals, departures, and foot traffic in a controlled area for a few hours. The utility piece is about protecting workers from vehicle traffic over several days, which WorkSafeBC's Part 18 of the OHS Regulation frames as a traffic control obligation tied to how long the work lasts and what risk assessment it requires. Treating both as one undifferentiated 'traffic control' request risks under-describing one of them when a request for support goes out.

Separating the two requests before contacting anyone

The practical move is to write two short scope summaries instead of one blended paragraph. For the event portion: exact street segment, event hours, expected pedestrian volumes, vendor and loading needs, and whether any travel lane will be closed to vehicles. For the utility portion: work dates and hours, whether work is short duration or spans multiple days, equipment movements, and whether an approved traffic control plan already exists for that dig.

The City of Vancouver's permit process, as described on Safeside Traffic Control Ltd's own service page, treats sidewalk, bus lane, traffic lane, and bike lane occupation as situations that may require a construction street-use permit, and the specific review path depends on the complexity of the closure. That means the event organizer and the utility contractor may each be dealing with separate permit conditions, even though their work is happening the same day within a few hundred metres of each other.

Once the two scopes are separated, matching them to a service type becomes straightforward. A short event with pedestrian and parking guidance typically points toward event traffic control support. A multi-day utility dig with lane or equipment exposure points toward construction traffic control, and if any travel lane is actually closed rather than just narrowed, that detail should be flagged explicitly as a lane-closure consideration tied to the permit, not assumed to be included automatically.

Deciding whether a written plan is needed or flagging alone will do

The decision point that actually matters here is whether either piece of work crosses the threshold where a documented traffic control plan is expected rather than flagging support alone. WorkSafeBC defines traffic control plans as workplace-specific documents describing control measures, how they will be implemented, and on what schedule, and the requirement scales with factors like duration and whether work is short duration, long duration, or mobile. A multi-day utility trench is more likely to sit in that documented-plan territory than a single-afternoon event with simple pedestrian guidance.

For the event side, if the street segment stays open to vehicles and the work is limited to guiding people and parking, flagging-style support focused on pedestrian and loading movement may be sufficient, provided the organizer still confirms permit conditions with the City. For the utility side, if the dig has already run multiple days and will continue, the contractor should already have, or be working toward, a traffic control plan rather than relying on ad hoc flagging, since the regulation ties the level of control to a risk assessment the employer is responsible for completing.

The worked takeaway is this: write the two scopes separately, check each one against its own permit and duration questions, and only then decide which service category, or combination, to describe when requesting support. A request that says 'traffic control for a street event near utility work' without separating the two risks a mismatched crew size, missed lane-closure permit step, or missing plan for the longer-duration piece.

Questions this guide answers

What if an event and a nearby construction project share the same date?

Treat them as two separate scopes even if they are close together. Each has its own hours, permit conditions, and risk profile, and WorkSafeBC's traffic control requirements are tied to the specific work activity and its duration, not to the calendar date alone. Combining them into one vague description risks leaving one side understaffed or missing a required plan.

How do you know if flagging alone is enough versus needing a written plan?

The regulation ties the level of control to a risk assessment and factors like how long the work lasts and whether it is short duration, long duration, or mobile work. Short, low-exposure tasks may be managed with flagging, while longer or higher-risk work typically needs a documented traffic control plan. When in doubt, the duration and permit status should be confirmed before assuming flagging alone will cover the scope.

Does a lane closure change which service category applies?

Yes, because closing a travel lane, bike lane, or sidewalk can trigger a different City of Vancouver permit review path than curb-lane or non-closure work. If either the event or the construction portion of a mixed scenario actually closes a lane rather than narrowing it, that detail should be called out separately when requesting support.
