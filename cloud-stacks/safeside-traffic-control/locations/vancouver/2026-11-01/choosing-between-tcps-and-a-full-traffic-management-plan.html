Choosing Between TCPs and a Full Traffic-Management Plan

A worked scenario for deciding whether a Vancouver project needs traffic control persons alone or a documented traffic-management plan.

A project owner planning curb-lane utility work has to decide early whether the job can run on traffic control persons alone or whether it needs a written traffic-management plan behind them. This decision shapes the permit conversation, the crew size, and how much lead time the project needs before mobilization. The right answer depends on how long the work lasts, how it interacts with the road network, and what the risk assessment finds, not on preference or convenience.

Walk through a representative scenario: a contractor needs to replace a section of water main under a curb lane on a secondary Vancouver street for roughly six hours in daylight. The crew assumes a couple of flaggers will handle it. Before confirming that assumption, the owner needs to work through the same questions WorkSafeBC requires of any employer managing traffic risk, because the regulation does not treat duration or visibility as optional details.

Start with the risk assessment, not the staffing guess

WorkSafeBC's Part 18 requires a risk assessment by a qualified person before any worker is exposed to traffic, and that assessment must consider whether the work is emergent, brief duration, short duration, or long duration under the regulation's definitions. In the water-main scenario, six hours of daytime work on a single street falls into short or long duration territory depending on whether it extends past one daylight period, which immediately changes what documentation is expected.

This matters because the regulation does not let an employer jump straight to picking traffic control persons. The hierarchy runs elimination first, engineering controls second, administrative controls third, and traffic control persons only where those aren't sufficient, as WorkSafeBC's guidance on traffic hazards describes. A project owner who skips this sequence and simply requests flaggers may be requesting more field labour than the risk assessment would actually support, or may be relying on TCPs where a barrier or detour would have reduced exposure more effectively.

The practical decision point: ask whoever prepares the risk assessment to state explicitly which category the work falls into and which control layer was selected and why. If that answer isn't available yet, the project isn't ready to specify a crew.

When a documented traffic control plan becomes necessary

A traffic control plan becomes necessary once the risk assessment identifies controls that need to be formally recorded on schedule, not just carried out informally. The regulation defines a traffic control plan as a workplace-specific document describing the measures, how they'll be implemented, and on what schedule, which is a different deliverable than simply assigning TCPs to a corner.

In the water-main example, if the work extends into a second day, involves mobile equipment moving along the block, or affects a bike lane or bus route, the project has moved from a simple flagging job into territory where the BC Traffic Management Manual's layouts and a Category-level traffic management plan likely apply. The Ministry of Transportation and Infrastructure's manual provides layouts for utility work, bicycle lanes, and multilane roadways specifically because generic flagging setups don't address those conditions.

The decision test for the project owner is this: if the work will run more than one daylight period, involves night work, is mobile, or touches a protected cycling route or transit lane, assume a documented plan is needed and confirm it with the road authority before booking crew. If none of those apply and the work is brief and contained to one lane with full sightlines, TCP-only staffing under a simpler risk assessment may be appropriate, but that conclusion should come from the assessment, not from guessing at the start.

One more branch worth checking: if the work qualifies as brief duration (15 minutes or less) or emergent (an unanticipated quick response under 5 minutes), the regulation allows a simpler determination process than the full written risk assessment and plan required for longer work. Confirming which bucket applies early prevents over-building or under-building the field response.

Matching crew training to the chosen control

Once the control layer is chosen, the people assigned to it must hold the training that matches the role, not just general safety awareness. Traffic control persons must complete training to WorkSafeBC's standard, delivered through one of the independently certified trainers currently recognized, which as of this review includes the BC Construction Safety Alliance's TCP program, BeeSafe, and Universal Health and Safety Inc. Training from any other provider does not satisfy the employer's obligation under the regulation.

A lower-risk variant exists for contained locations such as parking lots or vessel-loading areas, where a traffic assistant trained by the employer can be used instead of a fully trained TCP, provided that training covers workplace-specific hazards with both coursework and a practical component. This distinction matters for the decision scenario because confusing a traffic assistant with a traffic control person on a live roadway would leave the project out of compliance even if a person was physically present directing vehicles.

The closing check for the project owner: confirm the risk assessment outcome, confirm whether a traffic control plan is required given the duration and location, and confirm that anyone assigned to direct traffic holds training appropriate to that specific role and setting. Working through those three confirmations in order, rather than starting from an assumed crew size, is what keeps the staffing decision defensible if conditions at the site change and the plan needs review.

Questions this guide answers

Does short-duration curb-lane work always need a written traffic control plan?

Not automatically. The requirement depends on the risk assessment's findings and the duration category the work falls into under WorkSafeBC's Part 18 definitions. Short duration work still requires a risk assessment, but whether that assessment calls for a full written traffic control plan depends on the hazards identified, not the activity type alone.

Can a traffic assistant be used instead of a traffic control person on a public street?

No. Traffic assistants are intended for lower-risk, contained locations like parking lots or vessel-holding areas, while traffic control persons who direct traffic on roadways must complete the WorkSafeBC-standard TCP training. Using an employer-trained traffic assistant on a live traffic lane would not meet the regulation's requirement for that role.

Who is approved to provide traffic control person training in BC?

WorkSafeBC currently recognizes the BC Construction Safety Alliance, BeeSafe (a division of WorksafeT Solutions Inc.), and Universal Health and Safety Inc. as approved training providers. Training from any other source does not satisfy the employer's obligation to ensure TCPs are properly trained, so confirming the provider before the project starts avoids a compliance gap.
