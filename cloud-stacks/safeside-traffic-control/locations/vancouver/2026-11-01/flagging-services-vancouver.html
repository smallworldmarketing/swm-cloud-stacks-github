Booking Flagging Services in Vancouver: Questions to Ask First

Key questions to settle before booking flagging services in Vancouver, covering scope, qualifications, and how risk assessments shape crew size.

Booking a flagging crew in Vancouver is not just a scheduling task; it depends on what the project's risk assessment and permit conditions actually require. Some jobs need a trained traffic control person directing vehicles, others qualify for a lower-risk traffic assistant role, and the difference affects who you can hire and how the shift is structured. This article walks through the questions worth answering before a booking is confirmed, so the request matches the real field conditions rather than a guess.

Does this job need a traffic control person or a traffic assistant?

A traffic control person (TCP) is required when a qualified person's risk assessment determines that workers or the public face exposure to moving traffic that engineering and administrative controls alone cannot manage, and WorkSafeBC's framework treats TCPs as a control used only after other measures, such as barriers or scheduling changes, have been considered and found insufficient. A traffic assistant is a narrower role, limited to lower-risk settings such as parking lots or vessel-loading areas, and trained differently from a TCP.

Confusing these two roles before booking can lead to the wrong person showing up for the job. If the work involves directing vehicles on an open roadway, travel lane, or bike lane, the project almost certainly needs a properly trained TCP rather than a traffic assistant, and that distinction should be confirmed with whoever completed the risk assessment before any crew is requested.

What does the risk assessment and duration category change about the booking?

The duration of the work affects what level of planning is required. WorkSafeBC's Part 18 regulation separates work into categories such as brief duration, short duration, and long duration work, each with different expectations for how formal the risk assessment and traffic control plan need to be. Emergent, unplanned work has its own quick-response category as well.

For the person booking flagging support, this matters because a single-day short task and a multi-week long-duration closure are not interchangeable requests. A long-duration or night project is more likely to require a documented traffic control plan, additional signage, and possibly multiple TCPs working in communication with each other, while a brief duration task may be managed with a simpler arrangement. Asking which duration category applies, and whether a written plan already exists, helps set realistic expectations for crew size and shift structure before scheduling is finalized.

It is also worth asking what happens if conditions change mid-project. WorkSafeBC guidance notes that a risk assessment and its traffic control plan should be reviewed and updated whenever worksite conditions change, which means a booking made for one set of conditions may need to be revisited if the scope shifts after crews are already assigned.

Where should training and equipment questions fit into the request?

Training source matters because WorkSafeBC recognizes only specific approved providers for TCP training, and work completed by someone trained outside that approved list does not meet an employer's regulatory obligations. The B.C. Construction Safety Alliance's TCP Program is one of the established pathways for this training in the province. Anyone booking flagging support for a project they are responsible for should be comfortable confirming that the assigned personnel completed training through a WorkSafeBC-recognized provider, since this is a compliance question rather than a preference.

Equipment and visibility requirements also shift with conditions. The regulation distinguishes requirements for daytime operations from those during darkness or poor visibility, and it sets expectations for how control devices are maintained and placed. A booking request should specify expected hours of work, including whether any portion extends into early morning, evening, or low-visibility conditions, so that the equipment and staffing match those conditions rather than being adjusted after the crew has already arrived.

Finally, it helps to ask what information the crew will need on-site: the approved traffic control plan if one exists, the permit conditions attached to the street-use request, and a clear description of affected lanes, sidewalks, or bike routes. Gathering this before the shift is booked reduces the chance of a mismatch between what was planned and what the crew can actually implement once on site.

Questions this guide answers

Can any trained flagger work a traffic lane closure in Vancouver?

Only a traffic control person trained to WorkSafeBC's standard through an approved provider should be assigned to direct traffic on an open travel lane. A traffic assistant's training covers lower-risk settings like parking areas, not roadway traffic control, so matching the role to the risk assessment's findings is the deciding factor, and the specific provider used should be confirmed before booking.

Does a short one-day job still need a written traffic control plan?

Not automatically; WorkSafeBC's framework separates brief, short, and long duration work, and the level of documentation expected scales with that category and with the risk assessment's findings. A short daylight task may be handled with a simpler arrangement, but the only way to know is to confirm the duration category and any applicable permit conditions before the shift is scheduled.

What should change about a flagging request for night work?

Night or low-visibility work falls under different equipment and placement expectations than daytime work under Part 18 of the OHS Regulation. A booking request should state the actual hours of work up front so that signage, lighting, and crew arrangements are prepared for darkness rather than adjusted after the shift begins.
