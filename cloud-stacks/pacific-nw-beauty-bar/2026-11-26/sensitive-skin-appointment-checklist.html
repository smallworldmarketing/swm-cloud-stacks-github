What to Share About Sensitive Skin Before an Appointment

A practical checklist for describing sensitive skin, past reactions and current products before a facial or skin appointment, without self-diagnosing.

Deciding what to tell a provider about sensitive skin can feel overwhelming, especially if you have had reactions before and are not sure which details matter. The decision this checklist supports is simple: gathering specific, concrete information ahead of an appointment so the conversation with your provider is useful rather than vague. This is not a substitute for a provider's assessment, and it will not tell you whether a particular treatment is suitable for you.

Instead, think of this as preparation for a conversation. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that asking questions before a cosmetic procedure is one of the most useful things a person can do, and the same logic applies to facials and skin services (AAD, Cosmetic Procedures: Ask Questions). Bringing clear, organized information helps a provider understand your history and respond with relevant, realistic guidance rather than generic reassurance.

Build a short history of reactions and irritation

Start by writing down any specific products, ingredients or treatments that have caused redness, stinging, breakouts or prolonged irritation in the past. Include approximate timing, such as whether the reaction happened immediately or appeared a day or two later, since this distinction can matter to a provider trying to understand your skin's patterns.

It also helps to note whether reactions were linked to a new product, a change in weather, stress, or a professional treatment such as a peel or exfoliating service. The AAD's guidance on patch testing explains that trying a small amount of a new product on an inconspicuous area, such as the inside of the elbow, for a few days can help identify a reaction before it affects the face (AAD, How to Safely Test Skin Care Products). Mentioning whether you already do this at home gives the provider useful context about your current habits.

Finally, list any diagnosed skin conditions you are aware of, such as eczema or rosacea, along with any topical medications or treatments you currently use for them. You are not expected to interpret these conditions yourself; simply reporting them accurately allows the provider to factor them into the conversation.

List current products and routines in plain terms

A provider benefits far more from a simple, accurate list than from guesses about what might be causing sensitivity. Write down the cleansers, moisturizers, serums, sunscreens and any active ingredients such as retinoids or acids that you currently use, along with roughly how long you have used each one.

Include how often you exfoliate and with what method, since over-exfoliation is a common contributor to sensitivity. The AAD cautions that exfoliating too often or too aggressively can damage the skin's protective barrier, and recommends starting with less frequent exfoliation and adjusting based on how skin responds (AAD, How to Safely Exfoliate at Home). Sharing your current frequency, rather than assuming it is fine or problematic, lets the provider offer informed feedback.

Also note any recent changes, such as a new product introduced in the past few weeks or a recent professional treatment. Because reactions do not always appear immediately, a short timeline of recent changes is more useful to a provider than a single most-recent item.

Prepare specific questions rather than open-ended ones

Specific questions give a provider room to give specific answers, while broad questions such as 'is this safe for me' often produce only general reassurance. Consider asking what ingredients are used in a proposed service, whether any of them overlap with products that have caused you reactions before, and what signs would indicate you should stop or adjust mid-treatment.

It is also reasonable to ask how the provider typically handles a client who reports sensitivity, what a patch test or trial area might look like for a facial service, and what aftercare guidance they provide in writing. Clear answers to these questions help you make an informed decision without pressuring the provider into guaranteeing an outcome.

Finally, ask what to do if a reaction occurs after you leave, including how to contact the studio and what information they will want from you. Having this answered in advance, rather than during a reaction, makes follow-up far less stressful if something does come up.

Questions this guide answers

Should I stop using active ingredients like retinoids before a facial appointment?

This checklist does not set a universal rule, because whether to pause a product depends on your specific skin and the planned service, which is a decision for the treating provider. What you can do beforehand is list which active ingredients you use and how recently, so the provider has accurate information to work with during the consultation.

What if I don't know what caused a past reaction?

You do not need to know the exact cause before your appointment; noting the approximate timing, the products or treatments used around that time, and how the reaction looked and felt is enough. The provider can use that description, along with patch testing guidance from the American Academy of Dermatology, to help narrow down possible triggers.

Is a patch test something I can do myself before booking?

Yes, the AAD describes patch testing a new product on a small, inconspicuous area of skin for several days as a way to check for a reaction before wider use. This is a general consumer practice, not a clinical guarantee, so mention to your provider if you have already patch tested a product they plan to use.
