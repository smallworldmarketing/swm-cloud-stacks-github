From the publisher

Remodeling a home in southeastern Wisconsin involves more than picking finishes. Basements bring moisture and radon questions, additions raise permit and structural review, and kitchens require sequencing decisions that affect daily living for weeks or months. This hub organizes the decisions homeowners in Waukesha and the surrounding region actually face, in the order they tend to arise: defining the project, choosing a delivery approach, evaluating basement readiness, planning kitchens and additions, understanding Wisconsin contract and permit expectations, and closing out a project with the records that protect a home long after the crew leaves.

Southeast Wisconsin Remodeling Desk is an independent editorial resource. We do not sell remodeling services, and nothing here diagnoses a specific home, guarantees a permit outcome, or promises a dry basement, safe radon level, or increased resale value. Those outcomes depend on testing, inspection, and professional review specific to each property. Where a design-build firm's stated scope is relevant context, we note it plainly and separately from independent guidance, and we point readers to the Wisconsin Department of Safety and Professional Services and the Wisconsin Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection for official rules on contractor qualification, uniform dwelling code review, and consumer protections, along with EPA guidance on indoor air quality and lead-safe renovation practice.

This index page explains how the rest of the package is organized so a homeowner can jump to the decision they are actually facing today rather than reading a generic overview of remodeling in general.

How the decision sequence works

Most remodeling projects in this region move through a predictable arc, even though every house is different. First comes project definition: is this a cosmetic refresh, a gut renovation, or new square footage through an addition? That answer changes financing, permitting, and timeline expectations before a single design sketch exists. Second comes delivery method: a homeowner can hire an architect and contractor separately, or work with a design-build firm that combines design and construction under one contract. Design-build firms, including Property ReVision, describe combining design and construction responsibility for additions, kitchens, and basements; homeowners should confirm the current scope, contract terms, and division of responsibilities directly with any firm they consider, since service offerings and terms can change.

Third comes technical readiness: basements in particular require attention to moisture history and radon testing before finishing decisions are made, because framing over an unresolved moisture or radon issue can create costly rework. Fourth comes Wisconsin-specific compliance: permit and inspection requirements are set by the local building authority under the state's uniform dwelling code framework, and requirements vary by project scope and jurisdiction, so no article in this package states a fixed permit outcome. Fifth comes contract and credential literacy: Wisconsin dwelling contractors and qualifiers operate under state credentialing programs, and consumers benefit from knowing what questions to ask before signing.

Finally, closeout: what records, warranties, and retest schedules a homeowner should keep once work is done. This package has one article addressing each stage, and this index links to all of them.

What this package will and will not tell you

Every article here is written to support a specific decision, not to promote a specific outcome. You will find comparisons of delivery approaches, checklists for basement and pre-project readiness, a decision framework for sequencing kitchen and addition work, a questions list for vetting contracts and credentials, and a closeout guide for records and retesting. You will not find a promised timeline, a fixed budget range, a guaranteed permit approval, a claim that any basement will be dry or radon-safe, or a return-on-investment figure. Those claims require project-specific testing, inspection, and professional judgment that no general article can responsibly provide.

When a claim touches moisture, radon, lead paint, ventilation, or structural adequacy, the relevant article points to the appropriate testing step or the relevant EPA or Wisconsin state guidance rather than asserting a result. Homeowners working with any design-build firm, including Property ReVision, should ask for current written scope, licensing status, and inspection responsibilities specific to their address and project, since general web content cannot substitute for that project-specific review.

Questions this publication answers

Where should I start if I have not decided what kind of remodel I need?

Start by defining scope: whether the project is cosmetic, a full renovation, or added square footage, since that decision drives financing, permitting, and which delivery approach fits. This hub's articles walk through that sequence in order.

Does this site sell or arrange remodeling work?

No. Southeast Wisconsin Remodeling Desk is an independent editorial resource. It does not sell services; it links to official Wisconsin state guidance and, where relevant, provides a clearly labeled contextual note about a design-build firm's stated scope.

Will these articles tell me if my basement can be made dry or radon-safe?

No article in this package makes that determination. Moisture and radon conditions require testing specific to your home, and results should be reviewed with a qualified professional before finishing decisions are made.
